Exercise: Genomic intervals

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Convert coordinates between the 0-based half-open (BED) and 1-based closed (GTF, VCF, GRanges) conventions, by hand.
  • Read a narrowPeak line: width, summit, and which columns are statistics.
  • Build strand-aware TSS and promoter files from a GENCODE GTF with awk and bedtools slop.
  • Answer “what fraction of my peaks sit at promoters?” with bedtools intersect and with GenomicRanges, and get the same answer.
  • Test whether two sets of peaks overlap more than by chance with bedtools shuffle, and explain why the choice of background matters.

Background

Coordinates. BED and bedGraph count the boundaries between bases from 0, so an interval start end covers the bases after boundary start up to boundary end: length = end − start. GTF, SAM, VCF and GRanges number the bases from 1 and include both ends: length = end − start + 1. To go from 1-based [s, e] to BED: s − 1, e.

narrowPeak is BED6 + 4 columns: signalValue (enrichment), pValue and qValue (both as \(-\log_{10}\), −1 = not available), and peak, the summit as an offset from start.

Strand. The TSS of a transcript is its start on the + strand and its end on the − strand. Upstream of a − strand gene is towards higher coordinates.

Overlap counts and chance. Overlap by default means at least one shared base. Report each peak once (-u) when counting peaks. Whether an overlap is more than chance needs a null: shuffle one set to random positions (same widths) many times and compare.

Data

The exercise uses three GM12878 peak sets from ENCODE (H3K4me3, H3K27ac, CTCF; GRCh38), the GENCODE v46 basic annotation, the hg38 chromosome sizes, and the ENCODE blacklist. From the course repository:

data/download.sh genomic-intervals      # about 35 MB
mkdir -p ~/epi/intervals && cd ~/epi/intervals
D=/path/to/course/data                  # where download.sh put the files

You need bedtools and the usual Unix tools (see Install the software you need). Everything runs in seconds on a laptop or a login node; on the cluster you can also wrap the commands in an sbatch script.

By hand

Do these on paper.

B1. Coordinates.

  1. A VCF file reports a SNP at chr7 117559590 (1-based). Write the BED line for this single base.

  2. A BED line reads chr2 1000 1250. Write the region as a browser would display it (1-based), and give its length.

  3. A GTF exon is chr1 11869 12227 +. Write it as a BED line and give its length.

  4. Two BED intervals chr1 100 200 and chr1 200 300. Do they overlap? Do they share a base?

B2. A narrowPeak line. This is the first line of the ENCODE CTCF file:

chr12  53676107  53676353  .  1000  .  497.29450  -1.00000  4.87269  126

  1. How wide is the peak?

  2. At which position is the summit? Give it as a one-base BED interval and as a 1-based position.

  3. What does −1 in column 8 mean? What \(q\)-value does column 9 correspond to?

  4. Can you use column 5 to rank peaks by strength? Which column would you use?

B3. Promoters and strand. A transcript is annotated in a GTF as chr5 1000000 1050000 on the − strand.

  1. Where is its TSS (1-based)?

  2. Define the promoter as 2 kb upstream to 500 bp downstream of the TSS. Give it as a 1-based interval and as a BED line.

  3. A script computes every promoter as start − 2000 to start + 500, ignoring strand. Where does this put the window for this transcript, and what lies there?

B4. Interval operations. On a 1000 bp genome (chrT, BED coordinates):

A:  chrT 100 300    chrT 500 600    chrT 700 900
B:  chrT 250 450    chrT 850 950

Draw A and B on a ruler, then give the output of:

  1. bedtools intersect -a A -b B (b) intersect -u (c) intersect -v (d) subtract -a A -b B

  2. cat A B | sort -k1,1 -k2,2n | bedtools merge (f) closest -a A -b B -d (the distance column) (g) complement -i A with the genome file chrT 1000.

B5. Counting overlaps. bedtools intersect -u -a h3k4me3.bed -b promoters.bed | wc -l gives 21 390, but the same command without -u gives 63 214 lines. The H3K4me3 file has 53 912 peaks.

  1. What fraction of the H3K4me3 peaks overlap a promoter?

  2. How can the default output have more lines than there are peaks? Think about what a gene with many transcripts looks like in the promoter file.

B6. Overlaps by hand in R terms. Queries q1 = [1, 10], q2 = [20, 40], q3 = [50, 60]; subjects s1 = [5, 25], s2 = [35, 55] (1-based, as in GRanges). Write the result of findOverlaps(q, s) as a table of (query, subject) pairs, and give countOverlaps(q, s) and overlapsAny(q, s).

B7. Is it more than chance?

  1. 30 of your peaks overlap a set of enhancers. In 999 shuffles of the peaks, the mean overlap was 12, and 4 shuffles reached 30 or more. Compute the enrichment and the empirical \(p\)-value \((k + 1) / (N + 1)\).

  2. In another test none of 99 shuffles reached the observed value. What is the smallest \(p\)-value you can report, and how could you report the result honestly?

  3. A colleague shuffles peaks over the whole genome file, including chrY, unplaced contigs and the centromeres. Will the enrichment be too high or too low? Why?

  2. Which chromosomes appear? Is there a chrY in the CTCF file? Count the H3K27ac peaks on chrY. GM12878 comes from a woman: how do you explain what you find?

C2 — A TSS file from the GTF

zcat $D/gencode.v46.basic.annotation.gtf.gz \
 | awk -F'\t' -v OFS='\t' '$3 == "transcript" && $9 ~ /transcript_type "protein_coding"/ {
     match($9, /gene_name "[^"]+"/); g = substr($9, RSTART + 11, RLENGTH - 12)
     tss = ($7 == "+") ? $4 : $5
     print $1, tss - 1, tss, g, ".", $7 }' \
 | sort -u | sort -k1,1 -k2,2n > tss.bed
wc -l tss.bed
grep -w APP tss.bed

  1. Explain each part of the awk program. Why tss - 1, tss?

  2. APP (amyloid precursor protein, chr21) is on the − strand. Check that its TSSs are at the end of the gene: compare with the gene line, zcat $D/gencode.v46.basic.annotation.gtf.gz | awk -F'\t' '$3=="gene" && $9 ~ /gene_name "APP";/' | cut -f1,4,5,7. How many distinct TSSs does APP have?

  3. Replace sort -u | sort -k1,1 -k2,2n by sort -k1,1 -k2,2n -u. How many lines do you get now? Why fewer? (Read man sort on -u with -k.)

C3 — Promoters and the fraction of peaks at promoters

bedtools slop -i tss.bed -g $D/hg38.chrom.sizes -b 1000 > promoters.bed
for m in h3k4me3 h3k27ac ctcf; do
  echo $m $(bedtools intersect -u -a $m.bed -b promoters.bed | wc -l) $(wc -l < $m.bed)
done

  1. What fraction of each kind of peak overlaps a promoter (±1 kb of a protein-coding TSS)? Does the order match the table of marks in What is epigenetics?

  2. Run the same without -u for H3K4me3 (B5).

C4 — Strong peaks and weak peaks

Only about 40% of H3K4me3 peaks are at a protein-coding promoter, although H3K4me3 is “the promoter mark”. Rank the peaks by signalValue (column 7):

sort -k7,7gr h3k4me3.bed | head -10000 | bedtools intersect -u -a - -b promoters.bed | wc -l
sort -k7,7g  h3k4me3.bed | head -10000 | bedtools intersect -u -a - -b promoters.bed | wc -l

  1. What fraction of the 10 000 strongest and of the 10 000 weakest peaks are at a promoter?

  2. Give two reasons why a real H3K4me3 peak could be far from a protein-coding TSS. (Hint: what else has a promoter? What does the weak end of a peak list contain?)

C5 — Candidate enhancers and distances to TSSs

bedtools intersect -v -a h3k27ac.bed -b h3k4me3.bed \
  | bedtools intersect -v -a - -b promoters.bed > enhancers.bed
wc -l enhancers.bed
for m in h3k4me3 h3k27ac ctcf enhancers; do
  bedtools closest -a $m.bed -b tss.bed -d -t first | awk '{ print $NF }' | sort -n \
    | awk -v m=$m '{ a[NR] = $1 } END { print m, "median distance", a[int(NR / 2)] }'
done

  1. How many candidate enhancers are there, and what is their median distance to the nearest protein-coding TSS? Compare with the medians for the three peak sets.

  2. Which gene is nearest to the enhancer that is farthest from any TSS, and how far away is it? (sort -k17,17nr | head -1 on the closest output.) Is “nearest gene” a convincing target at that distance?

C6 — Merge and Jaccard

cat h3k4me3.bed h3k27ac.bed | cut -f1-3 | sort -k1,1 -k2,2n | bedtools merge > active.bed
wc -l active.bed
bedtools jaccard -a h3k4me3.bed -b h3k27ac.bed
bedtools jaccard -a ctcf.bed    -b h3k27ac.bed

  1. How many merged “active” regions are there? Why fewer than the sum of the two files?

  2. The Jaccard index is (bases in the intersection) / (bases in the union). Compare the two values. Does a small Jaccard index mean that CTCF and H3K27ac are unrelated? (Hold on to your answer for C7.)

C7 — Is the CTCF–H3K27ac overlap more than chance?

zcat $D/hg38_blacklist.bed.gz > blacklist.bed
grep -P '^chr([0-9]+|X|Y)\t' $D/hg38.chrom.sizes > genome.txt   # main chromosomes only

bedtools intersect -u -a ctcf.bed -b h3k27ac.bed | wc -l        # observed

for i in $(seq 1 100); do
  bedtools shuffle -i h3k27ac.bed -g genome.txt -chrom -excl blacklist.bed -seed $i \
    | sort -k1,1 -k2,2n | bedtools intersect -u -a ctcf.bed -b - | wc -l
done > shuffled.txt

for i in $(seq 1 100); do
  bedtools shuffle -i h3k27ac.bed -g $D/hg38.chrom.sizes -seed $i \
    | sort -k1,1 -k2,2n | bedtools intersect -u -a ctcf.bed -b - | wc -l
done > shuffled_naive.txt

  1. What is the observed number of CTCF peaks that overlap an H3K27ac peak? What is the mean over the 100 restricted shuffles, the enrichment, and the empirical \(p\)-value?

  2. Compute the same enrichment with the naive shuffle. Which is larger, and why (B7c)?

  3. Combine with C6: the overlap is highly significant, but what fraction of CTCF peaks overlap H3K27ac? Write one sentence for a results section that reports both.

Explore: the null decides the answer

The demo is the shuffle test of B7 and C7 on a simulated toy chromosome of 10 Mb. It has a gap with no sequence (like an unassembled short arm), a centromere, a few blacklisted repeats, and two GC-rich, gene-dense regions. Set A (150 intervals, blue) and set B (100 intervals, orange) both prefer the GC-rich regions and never fall in the gaps or the blacklist, as real peaks do. The statistic is the C7 one: how many A intervals overlap at least one B interval (bedtools intersect -u -a A -b B | wc -l). Shuffle B places the B intervals at random positions, keeping their widths, once, 10 or 100 times, and adds the counts to the histogram of the null; the red line is the observed count. Keep out of gaps, centromere and blacklist is bedtools shuffle -excl; match GC region keeps each B interval inside its own class (GC-rich or not), a matched background. The data switches between a data set where part of B really sits on A, and one where B only shares the GC preference. Each setting keeps its own list of shuffles; clear shuffles starts it again. Do B7 first.

E1. The naive null. Data: B is placed near A; both null boxes unticked. Press shuffle B ×1 a few times and look at the shuffled B row: where do the red-outlined intervals land, and roughly how many per shuffle? Then press ×100. Read the observed count, the null mean, the enrichment and the empirical \(p\)-value. Check the \(p\)-value with \((k + 1)/(N + 1)\).

E2. Keep out of the gaps. Tick keep out of gaps, centromere and blacklist and shuffle ×100. How do the null mean and the enrichment change? About what fraction of the chromosome did the naive shuffle wrongly allow (count the red outlines, or read the hatched part)? Relate to B7(c).

E3. Match the GC region. Tick match GC region as well and shuffle ×100. Enrichment and \(p\)? Press ×100 nine more times (N = 1000): what happens to the \(p\)-value, and why could it not go below 0.0099 with 100 shuffles? Is B associated with A?

E4. No association at all. Switch the data to B only shares the GC preference (the observed count drops). Run ×100 with the naive null (both boxes unticked) and then with both boxes ticked. Write the two conclusions a reader would draw. Which one is right, and what does this tell you about C7, where the null keeps peaks on their chromosome and out of the blacklist but does not match GC content?

Solutions

E1. The shuffled intervals fall everywhere, also in the gap at the left end, the centromere and the blacklist (about 15–25 of the 100 per shuffle, red outlines): places where no real peak can be. After 100 shuffles: observed 91 of the 150 A intervals, null mean about 39 (range about 23–56), enrichment 91 / 39 ≈ 2.3; no shuffle reached 91, so \(k = 0\) and \(p = (0 + 1)/(100 + 1) = 0.0099\). (If you pressed ×1 first, your numbers differ slightly.)

E2. The null mean rises to about 47 and the enrichment falls to about 1.9 (\(p\) still 0.0099). The excluded regions are about 19% of this toy chromosome; the naive shuffle put about a fifth of B where nothing can overlap A, so the shuffled overlaps were too low and the enrichment too high (B7c).

E3. Null mean about 72, enrichment about 1.26; with 100 shuffles, \(k = 1\) and \(p = 2/101 \approx 0.02\). With 1000 shuffles, 6 of them reach 91: \(p = 7/1001 \approx 0.007\). With \(N = 100\) the smallest possible value is \(1/101 = 0.0099\), so more shuffles are needed to show a small \(p\). Yes, there is a real association (30% of B were placed on A), but it is much weaker than the naive 2.3-fold suggested: most of the naive “enrichment” came from the shared preference for GC-rich regions and from the gaps.

E4. Observed 71. Naive null: mean about 39, enrichment about 1.8, \(p = 0.0099\): “B is strongly enriched at A”. Matched null (both boxes): mean about 72, enrichment about 0.99, \(p \approx 0.59\): “no association beyond the shared GC preference”. The second is right, because in this data B was placed without looking at A. The same can happen in C7: CTCF and H3K27ac peaks both prefer gene-dense, GC-rich parts of the genome, so part of the 6.7-fold enrichment may be shared preference rather than a direct link. A GC- or distance-matched background (regioneR, LOLA; the motif units) asks the sharper question. The null decides the answer: always state it.

Hints

Hint 1 — coordinates (B1, B2, R1)

From 1-based [s, e] to BED: start − 1, end unchanged. From BED to 1-based: start + 1, end unchanged. A single base at 1-based position \(p\) is the BED line p−1 p. For the summit: start + peak is a BED boundary; the summit base is start + peak to start + peak + 1 in BED. The narrowPeak format is described on the UCSC format page.

Hint 2 — strand (B3, C2, R2)

On the − strand the gene is read from right to left: the TSS is the largest coordinate, and “2 kb upstream” means 2 kb towards larger coordinates. resize with fix = "start" and promoters use the strand for you.

Hint 3 — bedtools (B4, C3–C7)

Draw it. -u reports each A once if it overlaps anything; -v reports A that overlap nothing; subtract removes the overlapping bases. merge needs sorted input. closest -d reports 0 for overlaps; for non-overlapping intervals bedtools reports one more than the number of bases between them. complement returns the gaps. sort -k7,7gr sorts on column 7 as a general number, largest first.

Hint 4 — shuffling (B7, C7)

Enrichment = observed / mean of the shuffles. Empirical \(p = (k + 1) / (N + 1)\), where \(k\) is the number of shuffles at least as extreme as the observation and \(N\) the number of shuffles. -chrom keeps each interval on its own chromosome; -excl keeps them out of the given regions. See bedtools shuffle and the ENCODE blacklist paper.

Hint 5 — GenomicRanges (R1–R4)

import with format = "narrowPeak" reads the extra columns. overlapsAny, findOverlaps and distanceToNearest are the R versions of intersect -u, intersect and closest -d. gr + 1000 widens a range by 1000 on each side. makeGRangesFromDataFrame has an argument starts.in.df.are.0based.

Solutions

Solutions — by hand

B1. (a) chr7 117559589 117559590. (b) chr2:1001-1250, length 250. (c) chr1 11868 12227, length \(12227 - 11868 = 359\) (in 1-based: \(12227 - 11869 + 1 = 359\)). (d) They touch but do not overlap: the first covers BED boundaries 100–200 (1-based bases 101–200), the second 200–300 (bases 201–300). They share no base, and bedtools intersect reports nothing.

B2. (a) \(53676353 - 53676107 = 246\) bp. (b) Summit boundary \(53676107 + 126 = 53676233\), so the summit base is chr12 53676233 53676234 in BED, position 53 676 234 in 1-based. (c) −1 means not available: the IDR pipeline did not report a \(p\)-value. \(q = 10^{-4.87} \approx 1.3 \times 10^{-5}\). (d) No: column 5 is a display score (here 1000 for every strong peak). Rank by signalValue (column 7), or by the \(p\)/\(q\) columns when they are available.

Where this leads

  • From reads to coverage makes the peaks and bigWigs we used here from raw reads.
  • Differential accessibility merges peaks from many samples into a consensus set (C6) and counts reads in it.
  • Motifs, motif enrichment and TF footprinting uses windows around summits (B2) and GC-matched backgrounds, a better null than C7.
  • Linking regulatory elements to genes replaces “nearest gene” (C5b) with contacts and activity.
