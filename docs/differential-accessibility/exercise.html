Exercise: Differential accessibility

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Compute median-of-ratios size factors by hand, and predict what a global change in accessibility or a difference in signal-to-noise does to them.
  • Write and explain the design matrix of a paired experiment (~ donor + celltype), and say when pairing helps.
  • Read an MA plot and a volcano plot of peaks, and interpret a log2 fold change and an adjusted \(p\)-value.
  • Turn a public peak × sample count matrix into a differential accessibility analysis in R: decode the samples, lift the peaks to GRCh38, build a SummarizedExperiment, explore, run DESeq2 with collapsed technical replicates, shrink fold changes, and compare normalisations and designs.
  • Annotate differential peaks with their nearest gene, check them against known markers and against RNA-seq, and save the result for Motifs, motif enrichment and TF footprinting.

Background

The count matrix. One row per peak (a consensus set shared by all samples), one column per library, integer counts of fragments in the peak. From here on the analysis is the RNA-seq one: DESeq2 or edgeR.

Size factors (DESeq2, median of ratios). For peak \(i\) and sample \(j\) with count \(k_{ij}\) and \(m\) samples:

\[ s_j = \operatorname{median}_i \frac{k_{ij}}{\left( \prod_{v=1}^{m} k_{iv} \right)^{1/m}} \]

Peaks with a zero in any sample are left out of the median. Normalised count = \(k_{ij} / s_j\). The assumption: the typical peak does not change. TMM (edgeR) makes the same assumption; normalising by total reads assumes the total signal per cell is the same; background-bin normalisation (csaw) assumes that reads outside peaks are constant.

Design. ~ donor + celltype fits one baseline per donor and one cell-type effect shared by all donors: the cell-type coefficient is the within-donor log2 fold change. ~ celltype ignores who is who.

FRiP (fraction of reads in peaks) = reads in peaks / all reads. Expected count in a peak ≈ depth × FRiP × (that peak’s share of the reads in peaks).

Our definition of a differential peak, used again in Motifs, motif enrichment and TF footprinting: adjusted \(p\) < 0.05 and |shrunken log2 fold change| > 1. Positive = more open in B cells (B_up), negative = more open in CD4 T cells (T_up).

Data

ATAC-seq and RNA-seq count matrices from Corces et al. 2016 (GEO GSE74912 and GSE74246), the UCSC hg19 → hg38 liftOver chain, and the GENCODE v46 annotation. From the course repository:

data/download.sh differential-accessibility      # about 70 MB (plus the GTF, 40 MB)
mkdir -p ~/epi/diffacc && cd ~/epi/diffacc
D=/path/to/course/data                           # where download.sh put the files
  • corces2016_atac_counts.txt.gz: columns Chr Start End (hg19, 0-based starts, 590 650 peaks of 500 bp) followed by 132 libraries (77 from healthy donors, the rest from AML patients and bulk CD34+ cells).
  • corces2016_rna_counts.txt.gz: gene symbols × 81 RNA-seq libraries.

What the column names mean. From the GEO sample table of GSE74912 (each library is listed with its donor and cell type): Donor4983-CD4-9A-150120 is donor 4983, CD4 T cells, cell-type code 9, library A, sequenced on 2015-01-20. The early libraries have only donor and code: 4983-7A is donor 4983, code 7 (monocytes), library A. The codes are 1 HSC, 2 MPP, 3 LMPP, 4 CMP, 5 GMP, 6 MEP, 7 monocyte, 9 CD4 T cell, 10 CD8 T cell, 11 NK cell, 13 B cell, 14 CLP, 15 erythroblast. Libraries A, B (C) of the same donor and cell type are technical replicates (Corces et al. 2016, Supplementary Table 1).

Shortcuts, said plainly. We start from the authors’ count matrix, not from BAM files (counting is shown in the lecture and in ATAC-seq processing and peak calling), and the peaks were called on hg19 and are lifted to GRCh38 rather than re-called.

The R part runs in about 12 minutes in total and needs about 4 GB of memory. On the cluster, run the R part in an interactive job or wrap it in an sbatch script (Rscript diffacc.R).

By hand

Do these on paper.

B1. Median of ratios. Four peaks, three samples:

peak S1 S2 S3
p1 100 200 50
p2 40 80 20
p3 300 600 150
p4 20 320 10

  1. Compute the geometric mean of each row. (Hint: \(100 \cdot 200 \cdot 50 = 10^6\).)

  2. Divide each count by its row’s geometric mean, and take the median per column. These are the size factors.

  3. Compute the normalised counts of p4. Which peak changes, and in which sample?

  4. Compute size factors from total counts instead (column sums, divided by their geometric mean; a calculator helps). Why is S2’s factor larger than with the median of ratios, and what does that do to p1–p3 in S2?

B2. A global opening. A drug is expected to open chromatin. Both libraries are sequenced to 1000 fragments (a toy scale). Counts in four peaks and in the background (all reads outside peaks):

p1 p2 p3 p4 background
control 50 100 150 200 500
drug 80 160 240 320 200

  1. What is the FRiP of each library?

  2. Normalise with the median of ratios computed on the four peaks (two samples: the ratio drug/control of the size factors is the median of the per-peak ratios). What fold change do you report for each peak?

  3. Normalise instead by the background reads. What fold change do you report now?

  4. Normalise by total reads. What do you get?

  5. Give one biological and one technical explanation for the data. What extra information would let you decide between them?

B3. Paired or unpaired. Three donors give B cells and CD4 T cells (6 samples, ordered D1-T, D1-B, D2-T, D2-B, D3-T, D3-B, with CD4 T as the reference level).

  1. Write the design matrix for ~ celltype (columns: intercept, celltypeBcell).

  2. Write the design matrix for ~ donor + celltype (columns: intercept, donorD2, donorD3, celltypeBcell).

  3. How many residual degrees of freedom does each model have? What does the intercept mean in (b)?

  4. In the toy figure of the lecture, the paired test wins clearly. In the real data, 47 peaks have a significant donor effect and the paired model finds fewer differential peaks (36 703 vs 41 671 at padj < 0.05). Explain both observations. Which model would you report, and why?

B4. Technical replicates. Donor 5483 has two B-cell libraries, A and B, with 120 and 80 fragments in a peak.

  1. What count goes into the collapsed sample?

  2. A colleague instead keeps A and B as two samples in ~ celltype. How many “B-cell replicates” does the model think there are? Will the \(p\)-values be too small or too large? Why?

B5. How many reads per peak? A library has 40 million fragments and FRiP 0.25; the consensus set has 100 000 peaks.

  1. What is the mean number of fragments per peak?

  2. Another library has 10 million fragments and FRiP 0.4. Mean per peak? Which library gives more information per peak?

  3. A peak gets 5 fragments in a sample. Ignoring biological variation (Poisson), what is the relative standard deviation of that count? Would you trust a 4-fold difference between 5 and 20 fragments in two single samples?

B6. Read the MA plot. Look at the MA plots in the lecture (MLE on the left, apeglm-shrunk on the right).

  1. Why do the MLE fold changes fan out to ±8 at the left of the plot?

  2. Why does shrinkage change the left of the plot much more than the right?

  3. Where is the centre of the grey cloud? What would you suspect if it were at +0.7?

  4. Are there coloured points with small mean counts on the right panel? Why are there few?

B7. Interpret the table. Four rows of a DESeq2 result for B vs CD4 (positive = more open in B):

peak baseMean log2FC (MLE) log2FC (shrunk) padj
a 1800 −2.3 −2.3 \(10^{-40}\)
b 4 6.1 0.4 0.30
c 250 0.8 0.7 0.001
d 90 3.0 2.6 0.02

  1. Which peaks are differential by our definition, and in which direction?

  2. How many times more open is peak a in CD4 T cells than in B cells?

  3. Peak c is significant but not differential by our definition. Is it “not changed”? What would you write about it?

  4. Why is peak b’s MLE fold change so large, and why is it not significant?

B8. Judge the claim. A paper reports: “Peaks more open in B cells are near genes such as PAX5 and CD79A, so these peaks drive B-cell gene expression. Moreover, 30% of B-specific peaks are near genes that are not more expressed in B cells, showing that accessibility and expression are uncoupled.”

Give two reasons why the first sentence overstates what was shown, and two reasons why a B-specific peak can sit next to a gene that is not differentially expressed.

Explore: normalisation under a global change

The demo simulates 3 control and 3 treated libraries (about 2 million reads each) over 2000 peaks, 2000 background bins and a spike-in added in a fixed amount per cell. Because the simulation knows how much material each cell had, it can show the true size factor of every library (red lines in panel b). Scenario sets the biology: a fraction of the peaks changes by a common log2 fold change (a global loss, as for H3K27me3 after an EZH2 inhibitor, or a global gain, as for a drug that opens chromatin everywhere), and 100 peaks are always truly up 4-fold. Extra background in treated adds unenriched DNA to the treated libraries (dead cells, a poorer transposition or IP): their FRiP drops without any change in the cells. Normalise by picks the size factors: total reads, median of ratios on the peaks (DESeq2), TMM on the peaks (edgeR), median of ratios on the background bins (csaw-like), or spike-in reads. Panel (a) is the MA plot, coloured by the simulated truth; solid points are called at BH 5% (a moderated t-test on log counts, simpler than DESeq2), and red rings are false calls. Panel (b) counts the calls.

E1. Start with no global change. Click through the five normalisations. How close are the size factors to the true ones (red lines), and how many true and false calls does each give?

E2. Click global loss (EZH2 inhibitor): 90% of the peaks drop to 25% (log2 FC −2). With the median of ratios, where does the cloud of truly changed peaks sit in the MA plot, how many peaks are called down, and how many false calls up are there? Where are the 190 unchanged peaks? Compare the size factors of T1–T3 with the true ones. Then switch to TMM, total reads, background bins and spike-in.

E3. Click noisier treated libraries only: no peak changes except the 100 truly up ones, but the treated libraries carry twice as much background. Read the FRiP of each library. Which normalisations now give hundreds of false calls, and in which direction? Why is the error larger with background bins than with total reads?

E4. Click loss + noisier libraries. Which normalisation still gives the right answer? Which assumption does each of the others break? This unit is ATAC-seq, but Annotation and differential binding makes the same argument for ChIP-seq: what would a peak-based normalisation report for an H3K27me3 ChIP after EZH2 inhibition, and what does the experiment need?

Solutions

E1. With no global change all five agree with the truth to within a few percent (the spike-in factors scatter most, because of the 5% pipetting error simulated for each sample) and all find the 100 truly up peaks, with at most one false call. When nothing global happens and FRiP is similar, the choice of normalisation hardly matters.

E2. Median of ratios assumes that the typical peak is unchanged. Here the typical peak did change, so the method makes it unchanged: the 1710 truly lost peaks sit around log2 FC 0 (median log2 FC of all peaks −0.07), only 6 are called down, and the 190 unchanged peaks are pushed up to about +2, giving 191 false calls up (the 100 truly up peaks now sit at +4). The treated size factors (0.74, 0.44, 0.61) are about half the true ones (1.43, 0.85, 1.18). TMM makes the same assumption and gives the same answer (191 false up). Spike-in and background bins recover the truth: 1708 of 1810 changed peaks called down, 100 true up, one false call. Total reads lands in between (1706 down, 16 false up, median log2 FC −1.78 instead of −2): the loss also lowers FRiP from 29% to 16–19%, so the total is only partly driven by the peaks.

E3. FRiP is 29% in the control libraries and 17–20% in the treated ones. Median of ratios, TMM and spike-in are right (no false calls). Total reads gives 1675 false calls down and background bins 1823. With more background, a treated library spends fewer reads in peaks, so per total read its peaks look lower. Background bins are worse because their assumption (background is the same in every library) is exactly what was broken: the treated size factor follows the inflated background, and every peak is pushed down by the full factor 2.

E4. Only the spike-in is right (1707 down, 100 up, 2 false up): it counts reads per cell, whatever happens to the peaks or the background. Median of ratios and TMM assume most peaks are unchanged (189 false up, 5 down); total reads and background bins assume the background per cell is the same (132 and 185 false down). For ChIP-seq this is the case of the Annotation and differential binding lecture: after an EZH2 inhibitor, peak-based normalisation hides the loss of H3K27me3 and makes the few unchanged sites look gained; an IP that worked less well behaves like the extra background here. Plan spike-in chromatin (ChIP-Rx) before the experiment, or measure the total amount of the mark (western blot, mass spectrometry). For ATAC-seq, spike-ins are not yet routine, so think about whether a global change is plausible and report how the result depends on the normalisation (R7).

In R

You need DESeq2, edgeR, apeglm, GenomicRanges, rtracklayer, data.table, ggplot2 and ggrepel (see Install the software you need).

suppressPackageStartupMessages({
  library(data.table); library(GenomicRanges); library(rtracklayer)
  library(SummarizedExperiment); library(DESeq2); library(edgeR); library(ggplot2); library(ggrepel)
})
D <- "/path/to/course/data"

R1 — Load and decode the samples

atac <- fread(file.path(D, "corces2016_atac_counts.txt.gz"))        # about 5 s
dim(atac)
cn <- names(atac)[-(1:3)]
healthy <- grep("^(Donor)?[0-9]+-", cn, value = TRUE)
re <- "^(?:Donor)?([0-9]+)-(?:[A-Za-z][A-Za-z0-9]*-)?([0-9]+)([A-C]?)(?:-[0-9]+)?$"
m  <- regmatches(healthy, regexec(re, healthy, perl = TRUE))
code2type <- c(`1` = "HSC", `2` = "MPP", `3` = "LMPP", `4` = "CMP", `5` = "GMP", `6` = "MEP",
               `7` = "Mono", `9` = "CD4", `10` = "CD8", `11` = "NK", `13` = "Bcell",
               `14` = "CLP", `15` = "Ery")
samples <- data.frame(sample = healthy, donor = sapply(m, `[`, 2),
                      celltype = unname(code2type[sapply(m, `[`, 3)]), rep = sapply(m, `[`, 4))
table(samples$celltype, samples$donor)

  1. Explain the regular expression piece by piece. Which part captures the donor, the cell-type code and the replicate letter? Why must the optional cell-type word start with a letter? (Try what happens to Donor7256-1A-110614 if it may start with a digit.)

  2. How many healthy libraries, donors and cell types are there? Which donors have both B cells and CD4 T cells?

  3. Which columns did grep leave out, and what are they?

R2 — Lift the peaks to GRCh38

gr19 <- GRanges(atac$Chr, IRanges(atac$Start + 1, atac$End))
chainf <- tempfile(fileext = ".chain")
writeLines(readLines(gzfile(file.path(D, "hg19ToHg38.over.chain.gz"))), chainf)
lo <- liftOver(gr19, import.chain(chainf))
table(pmin(lengths(lo), 2))                                     # 0, 1, 2 or more pieces
keep <- which(lengths(lo) == 1)
gr38 <- unlist(lo[keep])
ok <- width(gr38) == 500 & as.character(seqnames(gr38)) == as.character(seqnames(gr19)[keep])
sum(!ok)
keep <- keep[ok]; gr38 <- gr38[ok]
names(gr38) <- sprintf("peak%06d", keep)
length(gr38)

  1. How many peaks fail, how many split into several pieces, and how many single pieces change width or chromosome? What fraction is kept?

  2. Why atac$Start + 1?

  3. Why name the peaks by their row number in the original file?

R3 — A SummarizedExperiment of the eight libraries

sel <- samples[samples$celltype %in% c("Bcell", "CD4") & samples$donor %in% c("1022", "4983", "5483"), ]
cnt <- as.matrix(atac[keep, sel$sample, with = FALSE])
rownames(cnt) <- names(gr38)
cd <- DataFrame(donor = factor(sel$donor), celltype = factor(sel$celltype, levels = c("CD4", "Bcell")),
                rep = sel$rep, row.names = sel$sample)
cd$unit <- paste(cd$donor, cd$celltype, sep = "_")
se <- SummarizedExperiment(assays = list(counts = cnt), rowRanges = gr38, colData = cd)
se
colSums(assay(se)) / 1e6
lcpm <- cpm(assay(se), log = TRUE, prior.count = 2)[rowMeans(cpm(assay(se))) >= 2, ]
round(cor(lcpm), 2)

  1. How many reads in peaks does each library have? Which is the smallest?

  2. What is the correlation between the technical replicates of donor 5483 (B cells; CD4 T cells)? Between the B-cell libraries of different donors? Between B and T cells?

R4 — PCA: all cell types, then our six samples

cnt_all <- as.matrix(atac[keep, samples$sample, with = FALSE])
use <- rowMeans(cnt_all) >= 10
dall <- DESeqDataSetFromMatrix(cnt_all[use, ], data.frame(samples[, 2:3], row.names = samples$sample), ~ 1)
vall <- vst(dall, blind = TRUE)                                  # a few seconds
plotPCA(vall, intgroup = "celltype", ntop = 10000)

dds <- DESeqDataSet(se, design = ~ donor + celltype)
dds <- collapseReplicates(dds, groupby = dds$unit)
colData(dds)
vsd <- vst(dds, blind = TRUE)
plotPCA(vsd, intgroup = c("celltype", "donor"))

  1. In the PCA of all 77 libraries, which cell types group together? Is there a donor effect you can see?

  2. After collapseReplicates, how many columns are there, and what are the counts of 5483_Bcell (compare with the two libraries in se)?

  3. In the PCA of the six samples, what separates on PC1? On PC2?

R5 — DESeq2 with the paired design

dds <- dds[rowSums(counts(dds) >= 10) >= 3, ]
nrow(dds)
system.time(dds <- DESeq(dds))
resultsNames(dds)
plotDispEsts(dds)
res <- results(dds, name = "celltype_Bcell_vs_CD4", alpha = 0.05)
summary(res)

  1. How many peaks pass the filter? Why “≥ 10 reads in ≥ 3 samples” and not “in all 6”?

  2. How long does DESeq() take? What is the typical dispersion (median(dispersions(dds)))? What coefficient of variation between donors is that?

  3. How many peaks have padj < 0.05, in each direction?

R6 — Fold-change shrinkage, MA and volcano plots

system.time(shr <- lfcShrink(dds, coef = "celltype_Bcell_vs_CD4", type = "apeglm"))
par(mfrow = c(1, 2))
plotMA(res, ylim = c(-8, 8), main = "MLE")
plotMA(shr, ylim = c(-8, 8), main = "apeglm")
cls <- ifelse(!is.na(res$padj) & res$padj < 0.05 & shr$log2FoldChange >  1, "B_up",
       ifelse(!is.na(res$padj) & res$padj < 0.05 & shr$log2FoldChange < -1, "T_up", "ns"))
table(cls)
vol <- data.frame(lfc = shr$log2FoldChange, p = -log10(res$padj), cls = cls)
ggplot(vol[!is.na(vol$p), ], aes(lfc, p, colour = cls)) + geom_point(size = 0.4) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c(B_up = "#2a6f97", T_up = "#e67e22", ns = "grey70"))

  1. How many B_up and T_up peaks are there?

  2. Compare res$log2FoldChange and shr$log2FoldChange for the peaks with baseMean < 20. What does shrinkage do to them?

  3. How many peaks would pass |MLE log2FC| > 1 instead? Why is the difference small here?

R7 — Three normalisations

tot <- colSums(counts(dds))
y <- normLibSizes(DGEList(counts(dds)))
eff <- y$samples$lib.size * y$samples$norm.factors
sf <- rbind(DESeq2 = sizeFactors(dds), TMM = eff / exp(mean(log(eff))), total = tot / exp(mean(log(tot))))
round(sf, 2)
dt <- dds
sizeFactors(dt) <- sf["total", ]
dt <- DESeq(dt, quiet = TRUE)

Hints

Hint 1 — size factors (B1, B2, R7)

Geometric mean of three numbers = cube root of their product. With two samples, the median of the per-peak ratios S2/S1 is the ratio of the two size factors. Normalised count = count / size factor. The DESeq2 vignette explains size factors; TMM is in normLibSizes (formerly calcNormFactors) and Robinson & Oshlack 2010; background bins in the csaw book.

Hint 2 — design matrices (B3, R5, R8)

model.matrix(~ donor + celltype, data) prints the matrix in R. Residual degrees of freedom = samples − columns. The intercept in ~ donor + celltype is the reference donor in the reference cell type. DESeq2 vignette: multi-factor designs; the likelihood ratio test compares a full and a reduced design.

Hint 3 — replicates (B4, R3, R4)

collapseReplicates sums the counts of columns with the same groupby value. A SummarizedExperiment holds the matrix, the row ranges and the sample table together (vignette); DESeqDataSet(se, design) turns it into a DESeq2 object.

Hint 4 — Poisson and depth (B5)

For a Poisson count with mean \(\lambda\) the standard deviation is \(\sqrt{\lambda}\), so the relative SD is \(1/\sqrt{\lambda}\). Real replicates add biological variation on top (the dispersion).

Hint 5 — MA plots, shrinkage and results (B6, B7, R6)

plotMA, lfcShrink with type = "apeglm" (Zhu et al. 2019). A log2 fold change of \(-2.3\) is a ratio of \(2^{-2.3}\). Shrinkage changes fold changes, not \(p\)-values.

Hint 6 — liftOver and annotation (R2, R9)

rtracklayer::liftOver returns a GRangesList; lengths() gives the number of pieces per peak. import.chain needs an uncompressed chain file. distanceToNearest gives the nearest TSS, as in Genomic intervals. The UCSC liftOver documentation explains the chain files.

Hint 7 — reading the data (C1, C2, R1)

The GEO sample list of GSE74912 gives donor and cell type for every library. data.table::fread reads gzipped files directly. In the regular expression, (?:...) is a group that is not captured, ? makes it optional; see ?regex in R.

Solutions — by hand

B1. (a) p1: \((10^6)^{1/3} = 100\); p2: \((64\,000)^{1/3} = 40\); p3: \((2.7 \times 10^7)^{1/3} = 300\); p4: \((64\,000)^{1/3} = 40\). (b) Ratios S1: 1, 1, 1, 0.5 → median (of four: the mean of the middle two) 1. S2: 2, 2, 2, 8 → 2. S3: 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.25 → 0.5. (c) p4 normalised: 20, 160, 20. p4 is 8-fold more open in S2; p1–p3 are identical in all samples after normalisation. (d) Column sums 460, 1200, 230; geometric mean \((460 \cdot 1200 \cdot 230)^{1/3} \approx 503\); factors 0.92, 2.39, 0.46. S2’s total includes the extra reads in p4, so its factor is too large: p1–p3 in S2 become \(200/2.39 = 84\) instead of 100, a false 16% decrease, and p4’s increase shrinks from 8-fold to about 6.1-fold (21.9 in S1, 134 in S2). B2. (a) Control FRiP \(500/1000 = 0.5\); drug \(800/1000 = 0.8\). (b) Per-peak ratios drug/control are all 1.6, so the size-factor ratio is 1.6, and every normalised fold change is 1: no differential peak. (c) Background ratio \(200/500 = 0.4\); normalised fold change \(1.6/0.4 =\) 4 for every peak: a global 4-fold opening. (d) Totals are equal (1000), so the fold change is the raw 1.6. (e) Biological: the drug opens chromatin everywhere (e.g. an HDAC inhibitor), so the true answer is close to (c). Technical: the control library has more background (more dead or damaged cells, worse transposition), so its peaks look weaker; then (b) is right and the “opening” is an artefact. To decide: QC per library (TSS enrichment, fragment-size distribution, mitochondrial fraction, FRiP across replicates), a spike-in (a fixed number of foreign nuclei per sample), or an orthogonal measurement (e.g. imaging of chromatin compaction, or a set of loci known not to change).

B5. (a) \(40 \times 10^6 \times 0.25 / 10^5 = 100\). (b) \(10 \times 10^6 \times 0.4 / 10^5 = 40\). Between 5 and 20 the Poisson noise alone is large (and biological variation adds to it); a 4-fold difference between two single samples at this depth is weak evidence. This is what dispersion estimation and shrinkage handle.

B6. (a) Few reads: a peak with 0 vs 3 fragments has an MLE fold change that is huge and poorly determined. (b) apeglm shrinks in proportion to the uncertainty: weak peaks have large standard errors and are pulled to 0; strong peaks are well measured and keep their fold change. (c) At 0. A centre at +0.7 means the typical peak looks 1.6-fold more open in B cells: either a global difference (B cells more open overall) that peak-based normalisation should have removed (so check the normalisation), or a normalisation computed on a different set of peaks than the one plotted. (d) Few: a peak needs enough reads to reach |shrunk LFC| > 1 and padj < 0.05; weak peaks are shrunk below 1 or not significant.

B7. (a) a: T_up (padj tiny, shrunk −2.3); d: B_up (padj 0.02, shrunk 2.6). b and c are not. (b) \(2^{2.3} \approx 4.9\) times more open in CD4 T cells. (c) No: it is significantly more open in B cells, by about \(2^{0.7} \approx 1.6\)-fold. It fails only our effect-size threshold. Write “modestly more accessible (1.6-fold, padj 0.001)” if it matters; the threshold is a convention for making lists, not a test of “no change”. (d) baseMean 4: a few reads, so the MLE is noisy and huge; the standard error is also huge, so the Wald test is not significant and apeglm shrinks it to 0.4.

B8. First sentence: “near” a gene is not “regulating” it (the nearest gene is a guess, often wrong for distal elements); and correlation of accessibility and expression is not causation (“drive” needs perturbation, e.g. CRISPR deletion or inhibition of the element). A B-specific peak next to a non-DE gene: the peak regulates a different, more distant gene; the gene is expressed in both cell types from other elements; the element is primed (open before expression starts, or a repressor binds); or the gene is not expressed in either because another factor is missing. 30% non-concordance is therefore expected, not evidence of “uncoupling”.

Where this leads

  • Motifs, motif enrichment and TF footprinting reads differential_peaks.tsv, extracts the sequences of B_up and T_up peaks (GRCh38) and asks which transcription factor motifs are enriched, using the ns peaks as background.
  • Linking regulatory elements to genes replaces “nearest TSS” (R9) with contacts and activity, and returns to the RNA concordance (R10).
  • Experimental design and QC covers spike-ins and FRiP, the tools for deciding between the normalisations in B2 and R7.
  • Single-cell ATAC (ArchR, Signac) uses the same consensus peaks and counts, summed over cells of a type (“pseudobulk”) for differential tests.
