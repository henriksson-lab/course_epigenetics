Exercise: Annotation and differential binding

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Annotate peaks relative to genes by hand and with ChIPseeker, and say what “nearest gene” does and does not mean.
  • Make profiles and heatmaps with deepTools computeMatrix and plotHeatmap, and read them.
  • Build a consensus peak set and a count matrix, and test differential binding with DESeq2, as in RNA-seq.
  • Compute size factors from library sizes, from peaks, from background bins and from spike-ins, and explain why they disagree.
  • Recognise copy-number differences in inputs and global changes that need spike-ins.

Background

Annotation. Each peak gets one category by priority (ChIPseeker default: promoter > 5’ UTR > 3’ UTR > exon > intron > downstream > distal intergenic), with “promoter” = TSS ± a window (here 1 kb), and its nearest TSS with a signed distance (negative = upstream, positive = downstream, relative to the gene’s strand).

Differential binding. Regions from all conditions (union of peak sets, or peaks found in at least n samples) → reads (fragments) counted per region and sample → normalisation → negative binomial test (DESeq2/edgeR) with a design formula, exactly as for genes in RNA-seq.

Size factors. DESeq2 divides each sample’s counts by a size factor \(s_j\). Median-of-ratios (default): \(s_j\) = median over regions of (count / geometric mean of the region’s counts). Library size: \(s_j \propto N_j\). Background: median-of-ratios on large bins without peaks. Spike-in: \(s_j \propto N_{\text{spike},j}\). Each assumes something is constant between samples: all reads, most peaks, the background, or the spike-in.

Data

ENCODE H3K27ac ChIP-seq of GM12878 (ENCSR000AKC, 51 bp) and K562 (ENCSR000AKP, 36 bp), Broad lab, two biological replicates each, and one input per cell line; ENCODE’s filtered GRCh38 alignments (MAPQ ≥ 30, duplicates removed), cut to chr21 by data/prepare-chip.sh.

You need bedtools, deeptools, samtools and the R packages DESeq2, ChIPseeker, txdbmaker, GenomicAlignments, rtracklayer (see Install the software you need; BiocManager::install(c("ChIPseeker", "txdbmaker"))). The command line takes about a minute, the R part about two.

Simplifications

Two cell lines are an extreme contrast with thousands of real differences, and two replicates per group is the minimum, not the recommendation (three or more). The two experiments also differ in read length and date: technical variables confounded with cell type. Everything is on chr21.

By hand

B1. Annotate peaks. Gene G1 is on the + strand with its TSS at 10 000, exons 10 000–10 300, 15 000–15 200 and 29 500–30 000. Gene G2 is on the − strand from 40 000 to 52 000 (its TSS is at 52 000). Using a promoter window of TSS ± 1 kb, “downstream” = up to 300 bp after the gene end, and ChIPseeker’s priority, give the category, the nearest TSS and the signed distance to it for peaks centred at: a 9 400; b 10 800; c 15 100; d 22 000; e 30 200; f 51 500; g 35 000.

B2. Nearest gene. Peak g of B1 is a strong H3K27ac peak. A paper lists G2 as its target gene. What would you need to believe that? Name two kinds of data that would support or refute it.

B3. Consensus regions. Condition A has peaks at chr1:100–600, 2 000–2 400 and 9 000–9 300; condition B at 450–900, 5 000–5 500 and 9 100–9 500.

  1. Write the merged union. How many regions, and which come from one condition only?

  2. A student counts reads only in condition A’s peaks and finds “no region gained in B”. Why is that unavoidable?

B4. Three normalisations. Two libraries, each 1 000 000 reads. Counts in four peaks: A = 100, 200, 300, 400 and B = 150, 300, 450, 600. Counts in background bins (all non-peak DNA): A 999 000, B 998 500.

  1. Compute log2(B/A) for each peak after library-size normalisation.

  2. Compute DESeq2-style size factors from the four peaks (median of ratios; with two samples the ratio of each peak’s count to the geometric mean is simple), and the log2 fold changes after this normalisation.

  3. Background-bin normalisation gives nearly the same factors as library size here. Why?

  4. Give one biological and one technical story that would produce these numbers. Which normalisation is right under each?

B5. Spike-ins. H3K27me3 ChIP-Rx, control and EZH2 inhibitor, two replicates each, the same amount of Drosophila chromatin per sample:

ctrl 1 ctrl 2 trt 1 trt 2
fly reads 40 000 44 000 120 000 116 000
reads in domain X 600 660 600 580

  1. Compute spike-in size factors (fly reads divided by their geometric mean) and normalised counts in X.

  2. What fraction of H3K27me3 did domain X lose? What would DESeq2’s default normalisation (if most domains behaved like X) have concluded?

  3. Why does the treated sample have three times as many fly reads?

B6. Copy number. In ENCODE’s whole-genome inputs, chr21 has 1.45% of the K562 reads but 1.17% of the GM12878 reads.

  1. What is K562’s relative chr21 copy number (relative to its genome average)?

  2. A chr21 enhancer is truly equally acetylated per copy of DNA in both cell lines. What log2 fold change K562/GM12878 would you expect after a normalisation that equalises the genome-wide totals? How could you correct it?

B7. Library size and FRiP. The GM12878 libraries have FRiP 0.55, the K562 libraries 0.40 (reads in the consensus regions / all reads). If every peak had the same amount of H3K27ac per cell in both, what median log2 fold change K562/GM12878 would library-size normalisation produce? Compare with the −0.33 observed in R3.

B8. Judge the study. “We treated cells with an HDAC inhibitor, performed H3K27ac ChIP-seq (one replicate per condition, no spike-in), normalised to reads per million, and found that acetylation increased at 1 200 enhancers and decreased at 3 400.” List the problems and propose a better design.

B9. Read the heatmap (lecture: Real data: H3K27ac in two cell types). (a) Why are the profiles of the “both” group higher than the cell-type-specific groups in both cell lines? (b) The “GM12878 only” rows are not completely empty in K562. Does that mean these regions should be tested? (c) Why are regions grouped by peak set here rather than by k-means clustering?

R2 — Count and test

bl <- import(file.path(D, "hg38_blacklist.bed.gz"))
cons <- import("consensus.bed")
smp <- c(GM_1 = "h3k27ac_gm12878_rep1", GM_2 = "h3k27ac_gm12878_rep2", K562_1 = "h3k27ac_k562_rep1", K562_2 = "h3k27ac_k562_rep2",
         GM_input = "input_gm12878", K562_input = "input_k562")
centre <- function(s) {                              # fragment centres: shift each read 100 bp along its strand
  r <- granges(readGAlignments(file.path(D, paste0(s, ".chr21.bam"))))
  r <- r[!overlapsAny(r, bl)]
  resize(resize(r, 200, fix = "start"), 1, fix = "center")
}
mid <- lapply(smp, centre)
cnt <- sapply(mid, function(m) countOverlaps(cons, m, ignore.strand = TRUE))
rownames(cnt) <- paste0("chr21:", start(cons), "-", end(cons))
colSums(cnt); sapply(mid, length); round(colSums(cnt) / sapply(mid, length), 3)
cd <- data.frame(cell = factor(c("GM12878", "GM12878", "K562", "K562")), row.names = names(smp)[1:4])
dds <- DESeqDataSetFromMatrix(cnt[, 1:4], cd, ~ cell)
dds <- DESeq(dds, quiet = TRUE)
sizeFactors(dds)
res <- results(dds, contrast = c("cell", "K562", "GM12878"), alpha = 0.05)
summary(res)
plotMA(res, ylim = c(-8, 8))

  1. Why count fragment centres rather than reads? What is FRiP for each library?

  2. How many regions are significantly higher in K562, and how many in GM12878? Which steps are identical to an RNA-seq analysis?

R5 — A global change, simulated

set.seed(42)
n <- 2000; mu <- rlnorm(n, log(100), 1)
change <- rep(0.5, n); change[1:100] <- 1                 # treatment halves the mark at 95% of the peaks
truth <- cbind(ctrl = mu, ctrl = mu, ctrl = mu, trt = mu * change, trt = mu * change, trt = mu * change)
depth <- 1e6 / colSums(truth)                             # every library is sequenced to the same depth
counts <- matrix(rnbinom(length(truth), mu = sweep(truth, 2, depth, "*") * 0.2, size = 20), n)
spike <- c(5000, 5000, 5000, 10000, 10000, 10000) * c(1, 1.05, 0.95, 1, 0.97, 1.03)   # fly reads: same fly chromatin, half the human
colnames(counts) <- paste0(c("c", "c", "c", "t", "t", "t"), 1:6)
cd2 <- data.frame(group = factor(rep(c("ctrl", "trt"), each = 3), c("ctrl", "trt")), row.names = colnames(counts))
d2 <- DESeqDataSetFromMatrix(counts, cd2, ~ group)
r_rle <- results(DESeq(d2, quiet = TRUE))
sizeFactors(d2) <- spike / exp(mean(log(spike)))
r_spk <- results(DESeq(d2, quiet = TRUE))
sapply(list(RLE = r_rle, spike_in = r_spk), function(r) c(down = sum(r$padj < 0.05 & r$log2FoldChange < 0, na.rm = TRUE),
  up = sum(r$padj < 0.05 & r$log2FoldChange > 0, na.rm = TRUE), median_lfc = median(r$log2FoldChange),
  unchanged_peaks_lfc = median(r$log2FoldChange[1:100])))

  1. Read the simulation: what is the truth, and why are the treated spike-in counts twice the control ones?

  2. What does each normalisation conclude? Which peaks does standard normalisation call “up”, and why?

Hints

Hint 1 — annotation (B1, B2, R1)

Check the categories in priority order; a peak in an intron within 1 kb of a TSS is “promoter”. For a − strand gene, upstream means larger coordinates. ChIPseeker: annotatePeak (tssRegion, genomicAnnotationPriority), plotAnnoBar; a TxDb from a GTF with txdbmaker::makeTxDbFromGRanges.

Hint 2 — consensus and counting (B3, C2, R2)

bedtools merge needs sorted input; DiffBind’s dba.peakset(minOverlap = 2) builds a consensus of peaks in at least two samples. countOverlaps counts per region; resize(resize(r, 200, fix = "start"), 1, fix = "center") turns each read into the centre of its 200-bp fragment.

Hint 3 — normalisation (B4–B7, R3, R5)

Median of ratios: divide each count by the geometric mean of its row, take each sample’s median (DESeq2 vignette; estimateSizeFactorsForMatrix). You can set your own with sizeFactors(dds) <- s. Background bins and efficiency bias: Lun & Smyth 2016 and the csaw book. Spike-ins: Orlando et al. 2014.

Hint 4 — deepTools (C3, C4, B9)

computeMatrix (reference-point / scale-regions; one group per BED file), plotHeatmap (--sortUsingSamples, --zMax, --kmeans), bamCoverage. In a GTF the 9th column holds key "value"; pairs; BED needs 0-based starts.

Solutions

Numbers from running the commands as written (bedtools 2.30, deepTools 3.5.1, samtools 1.21, R 4.6, DESeq2 and ChIPseeker from Bioconductor 3.23).

Solutions — by hand

B1. a 9 400: within 1 kb of G1’s TSS → promoter, −600 (upstream). b 10 800: in the first intron but within 1 kb → promoter (priority), +800. c 15 100: exon (exon 2 of G1), +5 100. d 22 000: intron, +12 000 from G1. e 30 200: 200 bp after G1’s end → downstream; nearest TSS G1 at +20 200 (G2’s TSS is 21 800 away). f 51 500: within 1 kb of G2’s TSS → promoter; G2 is on the − strand, so 51 500 is 500 bp downstream: +500. g 35 000: distal intergenic; nearest TSS is G2’s (17 000 away; G1’s is 25 000), and since G2 runs leftwards from 52 000, 35 000 lies beyond its 3’ end: +17 000.

B2. That an element 17 kb beyond the 3’ end of G2 regulates G2 rather than G1 or another gene further away. Support would come from 3D contacts (Hi-C, HiChIP, Capture-C) between g and G2’s promoter, from correlation between g’s acetylation and G2’s expression across many samples, and above all from perturbation (CRISPR interference or deletion of g changes G2’s expression). Nearest-gene assignment alone is a hypothesis.

B8. n = 1 per condition (no estimate of variability; no test is valid); no spike-in although HDAC inhibitors raise acetylation globally (reads per million would hide a global increase and could even turn it into apparent decreases at many sites); reads-per-million normalisation assumes the total is unchanged; “increased/decreased” by an unspecified cutoff rather than a statistical test; probably no input (copy number unaffected here, but background). Where this leads

  • Differential accessibility runs the same analysis on ATAC-seq counts, with more replicates.
  • Linking regulatory elements to genes replaces “nearest gene” with contacts and correlation.
  • Chromatin states combines several marks into a single annotation of each region.
  • CUT&RUN and CUT&Tag produce cleaner data for the same questions; the downstream analysis is the same.
