Exercise: From reads to coverage

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Decode base qualities and SAM FLAGs, compute a fragment length from a read pair, and compute CPM, RPGC and a fold change with a pseudocount, all on paper.
  • Run the full chain from raw FASTQ to a filtered BAM on real ChIP-seq data: fastp → bowtie2 → samtools (sort, index, flagstat, idxstats, MAPQ/FLAG filter, markdup) → blacklist removal with bedtools, and keep a table of how many reads survive each step.
  • Make normalised coverage tracks (CPM, RPGC, ChIP over input) with deepTools and look at them in IGV.
  • Read the BAM in R with GenomicAlignments, and check your track against the one ENCODE made from the same experiment.

Background

Phred quality. \(Q = -10 \log_{10} p\), where \(p\) is the probability that the base call is wrong; \(p = 10^{-Q/10}\). In FASTQ, Q is stored as one character: ASCII code − 33 (all current data) or − 64 (Illumina files from about 2008–2011, like the ones here).

SAM FLAG bits: 1 paired, 2 proper pair, 4 unmapped, 8 mate unmapped, 16 reverse strand, 32 mate reverse strand, 64 first in pair, 128 second in pair, 256 secondary, 512 QC fail, 1024 duplicate, 2048 supplementary. POS is the leftmost aligned base, 1-based. For a proper pair, TLEN = (rightmost base of the pair) − (leftmost base) + 1, positive for the leftmost read, negative for its mate.

Normalisation. CPM = count × 106 / (reads in the library). RPGC divides the coverage by the average coverage, reads × fragment length / effective genome size. A fold change over input divides depth-scaled ChIP by depth-scaled input; a pseudocount (usually 1) is added to both so that empty bins give 0 on the log scale instead of ±∞.

The filtering chain used in this course (the ENCODE recipe): MAPQ ≥ 30 and -F 1804 → mark and remove duplicates → keep the chromosomes of interest (no chrM) → remove reads in blacklisted regions.

Data

GM12878 CTCF ChIP-seq (ENCODE ENCSR000DZN, replicate 1, single-end 36 bp, sequenced 2011) and its input control (ENCSR000EYX, single-end 28 bp, 2009). To keep the exercise small, the FASTQ files contain only the reads that ENCODE aligned to chr21 or chrM against the whole genome: 390 097 ChIP reads and 476 372 input reads, untrimmed, with their original names and qualities (see data/prepare-reads.sh). The reference is chr21 + chrM of hg38. You also need the ENCODE blacklist, and for comparison ENCODE’s CTCF fold-change bigWig and peaks.

data/download.sh enrichment-alignment     # FASTQ, reference, bowtie2 index, ENCODE files
mkdir -p ~/epi/align && cd ~/epi/align
D=/path/to/course/data                    # where download.sh put the files

You need fastp, bowtie2, samtools (1.10 or newer), bedtools, deeptools, macs3 and IGV (see Install the software you need). The whole command-line part runs in about 2 minutes with 8 threads on a laptop or a login node; on a cluster, put the commands in a script and submit it with sbatch --cpus-per-task=8.

A shortcut, and what it hides

A real analysis aligns to the whole genome. Aligning to chr21 + chrM only would force reads from other chromosomes onto the most similar chr21 sequence; we avoid that by starting from reads that ENCODE already placed on chr21 or chrM. The consequence: alignment rates look almost perfect, library sizes are about 1% of the real ones, and some multi-mappers look more unique than they are (their other copies are on chromosomes we left out). The steps, commands and decisions are exactly those of a full analysis.

By hand

Do these on paper.

B1. Base qualities.

  1. A Phred+33 quality string reads II5#. Give Q and the error probability of each base.

  2. The first read of the CTCF ChIP file has the quality string eeecceee^aY^^…dLYddd. Why can this not be Phred+33? Decode e and L as Phred+64.

  3. A 36 bp read has Q30 at every base. How many errors do you expect in the read? And at Q20? Out of 380 000 such reads, how many contain at least one error at Q30?

B2. FLAGs. Decode each FLAG into its bits and say in words what the read is:

99 147 83 163 0 16 4 1123

  1. Which two of the first four belong to the same pair, and which strand is the fragment’s leftmost read on?

  2. Which of the eight reads are removed by samtools view -F 1804? Which by -f 2?

B3. Fragment length from a pair.

  1. Read 1: FLAG 99, POS 10 001, CIGAR 36M. Read 2: FLAG 147, POS 10 181, CIGAR 36M. How long is the fragment? What is TLEN in each of the two lines?

  2. A read with FLAG 83 has POS 5 300 and CIGAR 50M; its mate has POS 5 140. What is the fragment length, and the sign of TLEN in each line?

B4. Extending a single-end read. A single-end read has FLAG 16, POS 2 000, CIGAR 36M.

  1. On which strand is the fragment’s sequenced end, and at which position is the read’s 5’ end?

  2. You extend the read to a 100 bp fragment. Which bases does the fragment cover (1-based)?

B5. CPM and RPGC. After filtering, the ChIP BAM has 197 587 reads. A 10 bp bin at a CTCF site is overlapped by 120 extended reads.

  1. What is the CPM of the bin?

  2. The reads are extended to 100 bp and the effective genome size (chr21) is 40 088 619 bp. What is the average coverage, and what is the RPGC of a base covered by 120 fragments?

  3. A second library has twice as many reads and 240 reads in the same bin. What are its CPM and RPGC? What does this show?

B6. Fold change and the pseudocount. In 50 bp bins, after scaling the input to the ChIP’s depth:

bin ChIP input (scaled)
A 60 12
B 3 0
C 0 2
D 0 0

  1. Compute ChIP / input and \(\log_2\)((ChIP + 1) / (input + 1)) for each bin.

  2. Bins A and B have similar log2 ratios. Is B as convincing as A? What would you change to make bin B more reliable?

B7. Duplicates. Eight single-end reads on chr21 (POS, strand, CIGAR all 36M):

r1 1000 +    r2 1000 +    r3 1000 −    r4 1003 +
r5 1000 +    r6 1210 −    r7 1210 −    r8 1500 +

  1. Which reads would samtools markdup flag as duplicates? What is the duplicate fraction?

  2. Why are r1 and r3 not duplicates of each other, although they have the same POS?

  3. In the real ChIP data, 49% of the reads inside CTCF peaks on chr21 were flagged as duplicates, but only 4% outside peaks. Give two explanations. Why does this happen with single-end reads more than with paired-end reads?

B8. The order of the steps.

  1. Put these in a sensible order and give one reason for each position: blacklist removal, bamCoverage, adapter trimming, MAPQ/FLAG filter, alignment, duplicate marking, sorting.

  2. In the input, 66% of the aligned reads are on chrM. A colleague makes the input’s CPM track from the BAM with the chrM reads. Is the input signal on chr21 too high or too low? By how much, roughly?

  3. Another colleague makes the ChIP track from chip.sorted.bam (no filtering at all). Name two things that will look different in the track.

B9. Read the figures (lecture slides Real data: MAPQ on chr21 and Real data: the two strands around CTCF sites).

  1. In the MAPQ histogram, almost no reads have MAPQ between 2 and 29. Why is the distribution bimodal, and what does that imply for the choice of threshold?

  2. The 5’ ends of + strand reads peak about 50 bp to the left of the CTCF summits, those of − strand reads about 50 bp to the right. Estimate the fragment length. Why is there almost no read 5’ end within ±18 bp of the summits?

  3. After MAPQ filtering, a 5 kb satellite repeat on chr21 has no reads at all. A colleague concludes that CTCF does not bind there. Comment.

On the command line

C1 — Look at the reads

zcat $D/gm12878_ctcf_chip.fastq.gz | head -8
zcat $D/gm12878_ctcf_input.fastq.gz | head -4
zcat $D/gm12878_ctcf_chip.fastq.gz | awk 'NR % 4 == 2 { n++; len[length($0)]++ } END { print n; for (l in len) print l, len[l] }'
zcat $D/gm12878_ctcf_chip.fastq.gz | awk 'NR % 4 == 0' | head -100000 | fold -w1 | LC_ALL=C sort | uniq -c | head -5

  1. How many reads are there, and how long are they? What do the read names tell you about the instrument?

  2. Which characters occur in the quality lines (the last command lists the lowest ones)? Decide whether the file is Phred+33 or Phred+64 (B1b).

C2 — Convert the qualities and trim with fastp

Old Phred+64 files are best converted to Phred+33 before anything else. Each quality character moves down by 31 (@ → !, h → I):

for s in chip input; do
  zcat $D/gm12878_ctcf_$s.fastq.gz | perl -pe 'tr/@-~/!-_/ if $. % 4 == 0' | gzip > $s.fastq.gz
  fastp -w 8 -i $s.fastq.gz -o $s.trim.fastq.gz -h $s.fastp.html -j $s.fastp.json 2> $s.fastp.log
done
grep -A 7 "Filtering result" chip.fastp.log input.fastp.log

  1. How many reads did fastp remove from each file, and why? Were any adapters found? Would you expect adapters in 36 bp reads from sonicated ChIP fragments?

  2. Open chip.fastp.html in a browser. How does the quality change along the read? Compare with the input.

C3 — The genome index

The course data folder contains a prebuilt bowtie2 index for chr21 + chrM ($D/bowtie2/). This is how it was made (27 seconds with 8 threads; hours for the whole human genome, so for real work download a prebuilt one):

mkdir -p index
bowtie2-build --threads 8 $D/hg38_chr21_chrM.fa index/hg38_chr21_chrM > index/build.log
ls -lh index

C4 — Align, sort, index

for s in chip input; do
  bowtie2 -p 8 -x $D/bowtie2/hg38_chr21_chrM -U $s.trim.fastq.gz 2> $s.bowtie2.log \
    | samtools sort -@ 4 -o $s.sorted.bam -
  samtools index $s.sorted.bam
done
cat chip.bowtie2.log input.bowtie2.log
samtools flagstat chip.sorted.bam
samtools idxstats chip.sorted.bam
samtools idxstats input.sorted.bam
samtools view chip.sorted.bam | head -3

  1. What fraction of each library aligned exactly once, more than once, not at all? Why is the unaligned fraction so low here (see the warning box under Data)?

  2. What fraction of the aligned reads are on chrM in the ChIP and in the input? chrM is 16.6 kb, chr21 46.7 Mb: how many times more reads per kb does chrM get in the input?

  3. Decode the FLAG, POS, MAPQ and CIGAR of the first three alignments.

C5 — Filter, mark duplicates, remove the blacklist

zcat $D/hg38_blacklist.bed.gz > blacklist.bed
for s in chip input; do
  samtools view -b -q 30 -F 1804 -o $s.q30.bam $s.sorted.bam            # unique, mapped, primary
  samtools markdup -s $s.q30.bam $s.markdup.bam 2> $s.markdup.txt       # flag duplicates (1024)
  samtools index $s.markdup.bam
  samtools view -b -F 1024 -o $s.dedup.bam $s.markdup.bam chr21         # drop duplicates and chrM
  bedtools intersect -v -abam $s.dedup.bam -b blacklist.bed > $s.final.bam
  samtools index $s.final.bam
done
grep -E "^READ|DUPLICATE SINGLE:" chip.markdup.txt input.markdup.txt
for s in chip input; do
  echo $s \
    $(zcat $s.fastq.gz | awk 'END { print NR / 4 }') \
    $(zcat $s.trim.fastq.gz | awk 'END { print NR / 4 }') \
    $(samtools view -c -F 4 $s.sorted.bam) \
    $(samtools view -c $s.q30.bam) \
    $(samtools view -c -F 1024 $s.markdup.bam) \
    $(samtools view -c $s.dedup.bam) \
    $(samtools view -c $s.final.bam)
done

  1. Fill in the table: reads left after each step (raw, after fastp, aligned, MAPQ ≥ 30 and -F 1804, duplicates removed, chr21 only, blacklist removed), as numbers and as a percentage of the raw reads, for ChIP and input.

  2. What is the duplicate rate of each library? Where are the input’s duplicates? (samtools view -c -f 1024 input.markdup.bam chrM and the same for chr21.)

  3. How many reads did the blacklist remove? Which blacklisted regions on chr21 have the most input reads per kb, and how does that compare with the chr21 average (116 397 reads / 40 089 kb)?

bedtools intersect -c -a blacklist.bed -b input.dedup.bam \
  | awk -v OFS='\t' '$4 > 0 { print $0, $4 / ($3 - $2) * 1000 }' | sort -k5,5gr | head -3

C6 — How long are the fragments?

Our reads are single-end, so the fragment length must be estimated from the shift between the strands (lecture: Single-end reads need extending):

macs3 predictd -i chip.final.bam -f BAM -g 40088619 2>&1 | grep "predicted fragment length"

What fragment length does MACS3 predict? We use it for --extendReads below.

C7 — Coverage tracks

for s in chip input; do
  bamCoverage -b $s.final.bam -o $s.cpm.bw --normalizeUsing CPM --extendReads 100 --binSize 10 -p 8
done
bamCoverage -b chip.final.bam -o chip.rpgc.bw --normalizeUsing RPGC \
  --effectiveGenomeSize 40088619 --extendReads 100 --binSize 10 -p 8
bamCompare -b1 chip.final.bam -b2 input.final.bam -o chip_vs_input.log2.bw \
  --operation log2 --scaleFactorsMethod readCount --pseudocount 1 \
  --extendReads 100 --binSize 50 -p 8
bamCompare -b1 chip.final.bam -b2 input.final.bam -o chip_vs_input.ratio.bw \
  --operation ratio --scaleFactorsMethod readCount --pseudocount 1 \
  --extendReads 100 --binSize 50 -p 8
ls -lh *.bw

  1. Why is --effectiveGenomeSize 40 088 619 and not the length of chr21 (46 709 983)? (Hint: grep -v ">" $D/hg38_chr21_chrM.fa | head -100000 | grep -c N and think about what N means.)

  2. The RPGC and CPM tracks differ only by a constant factor. Compute it from B5 and check it in IGV at any peak.

C8 — Look at it in IGV

Open IGV, choose the genome hg38, and load chip.cpm.bw, input.cpm.bw, chip_vs_input.log2.bw, $D/gm12878_ctcf_fc.bigWig, $D/gm12878_ctcf_peaks.bed.gz and chip.final.bam. Go to chr21:43,775,000-43,825,000.

  1. How many CTCF peaks are in the window? Do your ChIP track and ENCODE’s fold change agree on where they are?

  2. Zoom in to the peak at about 43 789 450 until you see single reads in the BAM track. Colour the reads by strand (right click → Color alignments by → read strand). Where are the + strand reads, where the − strand reads?

  3. Why is your log2 ratio track so much noisier than ENCODE’s fold change away from the peaks? (Count the input reads in the window: samtools view -c input.final.bam chr21:43775000-43825000.)

Explore: from reads to a coverage track

The demo simulates single-end ChIP-seq reads in a 1200 bp window around one binding site: 150 fragments that carry the bound protein (so they contain the site), some background fragments, and an input sequenced at half the ChIP’s depth. Each fragment is read from one end only: blue reads are on the + strand (read from the fragment’s left end), orange reads on the − strand. Move the fragment length (the true mean length of the fragments) and the read length; extend reads to the fragment length extends every read from its 5’ end, in its own direction, as bamCoverage --extendReads does; add PCR duplicates gives a few fragments many identical copies, and remove duplicates keeps one read per start position and strand, as samtools markdup does for single-end reads. The track is the read count per bin, CPM, or the log2 fold change over the input with a pseudocount (as bamCompare); the bin size sets how wide each bin is. The numbers above the plot update as you move the controls. Do B4–B7 first.

E1. Two humps. Start from the default (fragment 200 bp, read 36 bp, not extended, raw counts, 10 bp bins; reset brings you back). (a) Read the centres of the + and − read humps. How far apart are they? Compare with fragment length − read length. (b) Repeat with fragment lengths 100 and 300 bp. (c) Why is the bin that contains the site much lower than the highest bin? Compare with B9(b).

E2. Extending. Back at 200 bp, tick extend reads to the fragment length. (a) Where is the highest bin now, and how high is it compared with E1? What is the peak width at half height? (b) Untick extension, and set both the read length and the fragment length to 150 bp. Is there still a dip at the site? Tick extension again: what changes? When does a coverage track need extended reads?

E3. Duplicates. Extension off, fragment 200 bp, read 36 bp, bin size 1 bp. Tick add PCR duplicates. (a) How many PCR copies were added, what do they look like in the reads and in the track, and what happens to the library size? (b) Tick remove duplicates. How many reads are removed, and how many of them were not PCR copies? Why are independent fragments removed too, and why does this happen most at strong peaks with single-end reads (B7c)?

E4. Units. Untick both duplicate boxes, tick extension, bin size 10 bp. (a) Switch from raw counts to CPM. Read the highest bin and check it from the raw count and the library size (B5). (b) Switch to log2 fold change over input with pseudocount 1. What is the value in the bin with the site, and roughly where do the flanks lie? Why there? (c) Set the bin size to 1 bp and the pseudocount to 0.1. Where is the highest bin now, and why is it not a real peak? What does pseudocount 5 do to the site’s value?

Solutions

E1. (a) The + hump is centred at −72 bp and the − hump at +87 bp: 159 bp apart, close to 200 − 36 = 164 bp. A + read covers the first 36 bp of a fragment that starts somewhere in the 200 bp before the site (its centre on average about −(200 − 36)/2 bp), a − read the last 36 bp of a fragment that ends after it. (b) 100 bp: −24 and +32 bp, 56 bp apart (100 − 36 = 64); 300 bp: −117 and +143 bp, 260 bp apart (264). The strand shift grows with the fragment length: this is what macs3 predictd and the strand cross-correlation measure. (c) The highest bin has 31 reads at −65 bp, the bin with the site only 14. Fragments that carry the protein start at least ~18 bp before the site (the footprint), so a 36 bp + read rarely reaches the site, and likewise for − reads from the other side: the site sits in the gap between the two humps, as in the real CTCF data of B9(b).

E2. (a) The highest bin is now the site’s own bin (+5 bp), with 154 reads instead of 31: every extended read covers its whole (assumed) fragment, so the two humps merge into one peak centred on the site. Its width at half height is 210 bp, about the fragment length. (b) No dip: with 150 bp reads on ~150 bp fragments the reads already cover the fragments, and the highest bin is the site’s bin, 153 reads at +5 bp, without extension, and extension changes almost nothing (153 at +5 bp). Extension is needed when reads are shorter than the fragments, i.e. for single-end data; for paired-end data the real fragment between the mates is used instead.

E3. (a) 111 PCR copies (red) stack on top of a few fragments: identical reads, same start, same strand. In the 1 bp track they make narrow spikes (the highest bin goes from 28 to 47 reads at −78 bp), and the library grows from 2.00 M to 3.28 M reads (the demo assumes the same duplicate rate genome-wide). (b) 142 reads are removed: the 111 PCR copies and 31 independent fragments that happen to share a start and strand with another fragment. At a strong peak, fragments that carry the protein can only start in a window of about one fragment length per strand, so with 150 of them many real starts coincide; removing duplicates therefore also compresses strong peaks (B7c: 49% “duplicates” inside CTCF peaks, 4% outside). With paired-end reads both ends must match, which is much rarer by chance.

E4. (a) 77.0 CPM = 154 × 106 / 2 000 000. (b) The bin with the site has log2 fold change 4.11 (154 ChIP reads against 8 depth-scaled input reads: log2(155/9)); the highest bin is 4.88 at −25 bp. The flanks lie between about −1.6 and +2.3, scattered around 0: there, ChIP background and input have the same density once scaled to the same depth, and with only a few reads per bin the ratio is noisy. (c) The highest bin moves to +185 bp, at 6.92: a bin where the input has no read, so the ratio is (ChIP + 0.1)/0.1 and a handful of background ChIP reads looks like a 100-fold enrichment. A pseudocount of 5 shrinks every ratio towards 0, the small-count bins most: the site’s bin drops from 4.09 (pseudocount 1) to 3.59, and the noisy flanks flatten. This is why ratio tracks use bins of 50 bp or more, a pseudocount of 1 or more, or a smoothed input (B6, C8c).

In R

You need GenomicAlignments (with Rsamtools), rtracklayer and GenomicRanges (see Install the software you need). Run R in the folder with your BAM files:

suppressPackageStartupMessages({ library(GenomicAlignments); library(rtracklayer) })
D <- "/path/to/course/data"
setwd("~/epi/align")

R1 — Read the BAM and look at MAPQ

aln <- readGAlignments("chip.sorted.bam", param = ScanBamParam(what = c("flag", "mapq")))
aln
table(seqnames(aln))
mq <- mcols(aln[seqnames(aln) == "chr21"])$mapq
table(cut(mq, c(-1, 1, 10, 29, 41, 42)))
mean(mq >= 30)
hist(mq, breaks = 0:43 - 0.5, main = "ChIP, chr21", xlab = "MAPQ")

  1. What fraction of the ChIP reads on chr21 have MAPQ 0 or 1, and what fraction MAPQ ≥ 30? Compare with samtools view -c -q 30 chip.sorted.bam chr21.

  2. Repeat for input.sorted.bam. Which library has more multi-mappers on chr21, and why might that be (read length)?

R2 — Duplicates by hand

A single-end read is a duplicate of another if they have the same chromosome, strand and 5’ end.

q30 <- granges(readGAlignments("chip.q30.bam"))
five <- resize(q30, width = 1, fix = "start")       # the 5' end, strand-aware
key <- paste(seqnames(five), start(five), strand(five))
sum(duplicated(key)); mean(duplicated(key))
tapply(duplicated(key), as.character(seqnames(five)), mean)

  1. How many duplicates do you find? Compare with DUPLICATE SINGLE in chip.markdup.txt.

  2. Why is the duplicate rate on chrM so much higher than on chr21?

R3 — Coverage at a CTCF peak

fin <- granges(readGAlignments("chip.final.bam"))

Hints

Hint 1 — qualities (B1, C1, C2)

\(Q\) = ASCII code − offset; I = 73, 5 = 53, # = 35, e = 101, L = 76 (see an ASCII table and the FASTQ format page). Phred+33 qualities rarely go above Q41 (J). The probability of no error in \(n\) bases is \((1-p)^n\). In the conversion, Perl’s tr/@-~/!-_/ maps the character range @…~ (ASCII 64–126) onto !…_ (33–95), only on every fourth line ($. % 4 == 0). fastp’s options are documented in its README; by default it removes reads with more than 40% of bases below Q15, reads with more than 5 N, and reads shorter than 15 bases after trimming.

Hint 2 — FLAG, POS, TLEN (B2–B4, C4)

Subtract the largest power of two that fits, repeat. The SAM specification (section 1.4) defines every column; the Picard explain-flags page and samtools flags 99 decode FLAGs for you. A − strand read is stored reverse-complemented: its 5’ end is its rightmost base, POS + length − 1. -F removes reads with any of the bits, -f keeps reads with all of them (samtools view).

Hint 3 — normalisation (B5, B6, C7)

CPM per bin = reads overlapping the bin × 106 / reads in the BAM. RPGC = coverage / (reads × fragment length / effective genome size). The deepTools pages for bamCoverage, bamCompare and effective genome size give the formulas and the defaults. bamCompare --scaleFactorsMethod readCount scales the larger library down to the smaller one before adding the pseudocount.

Hint 4 — duplicates, blacklist, order (B7, B8, C5)

samtools markdup compares the 5’ end and strand (single-end) or both ends (paired-end), and keeps the copy with the highest base qualities; -s prints statistics. For paired-end reads it needs samtools fixmate -m on name-collated reads first. The blacklist is described in Amemiya et al. 2019; bedtools intersect -v -abam writes the reads that overlap no blacklisted region (docs). For B8b: CPM divides by all reads in the BAM.

Hint 5 — fragment length and IGV (B9, C6, C8)

macs3 predictd pairs strong + strand and − strand peaks and reports the most common distance between them; -g is the effective genome size. In IGV, bigWig and BED files load with File → Load from File; a BAM needs its .bai index next to it.

Hint 6 — R (R1–R5)

readGAlignments with a ScanBamParam reads extra columns such as mapq and flag. granges() turns alignments into ranges; resize(..., fix = "start") is strand-aware, so the “start” of a − strand read is its right end. coverage returns an RleList per chromosome. tileGenome and binnedAverage make bins and average a signal over them; import with as = "RleList" reads a bigWig.

Solutions — by hand

B1. (a) I = 73 − 33 = Q40, \(p = 10^{-4} = 0.0001\); 5 = Q20, \(p = 0.01\); # = Q2, \(p = 10^{-0.2} = 0.63\) (a “this base is useless” marker). (b) As +33, e would be 101 − 33 = Q68, far above anything a sequencer reports (+33 data top out around Q41). As +64: e = 101 − 64 = Q37 (\(p = 2 \times 10^{-4}\)), L = 76 − 64 = Q12 (\(p = 0.063\)). (c) \(36 \times 0.001 = 0.036\) errors per read at Q30; \(36 \times 0.01 = 0.36\) at Q20. The probability of at least one error at Q30 is \(1 - 0.999^{36} = 0.035\), so about 13 400 of 380 000 reads. Aligners tolerate this with mismatches.

B2. 99 = 64 + 32 + 2 + 1: read 1, mate reverse, proper pair. 147 = 128 + 16 + 2 + 1: read 2, reverse strand, proper pair. 83 = 64 + 16 + 2 + 1: read 1, reverse strand, proper pair. 163 = 128 + 32 + 2 + 1: read 2, mate reverse, proper pair (so read 2 is forward). 0: single-end, mapped, + strand. 16: single-end, − strand. 4: unmapped. 1123 = 1024 + 64 + 32 + 2 + 1: like 99, but flagged as a duplicate. (a) 99 and 147 are a pair (read 1 forward on the left, read 2 reverse on the right); 83 and 163 are a pair (read 2 forward on the left, read 1 reverse on the right). (b) -F 1804 removes 4 (unmapped) and 1123 (duplicate). -f 2 removes 0, 16, 4 (not proper pairs: single-end) and keeps the four proper-pair reads and 1123.

B3. (a) Read 2 ends at 10 181 + 36 − 1 = 10 216. Fragment = 10 216 − 10 001 + 1 = 216 bp; TLEN = +216 for read 1, −216 for read 2. (b) The mate (POS 5 140) is leftmost; the FLAG-83 read ends at 5 300 + 50 − 1 = 5 349. Fragment = 5 349 − 5 140 + 1 = 210 bp; TLEN is −210 in the FLAG-83 line and +210 in its mate’s line.

B4. (a) The − strand: the read covers 2 000–2 035, and its 5’ end (where sequencing started, the end of the fragment) is at 2 035. (b) Extending towards smaller coordinates: 1 936–2 035.

B5. (a) \(120 \times 10^6 / 197\,587 = 607\) CPM. (b) Average coverage \(197\,587 \times 100 / 40\,088\,619 = 0.493\); RPGC \(= 120 / 0.493 = 243\). (c) The same: \(240 \times 10^6 / 395\,174 = 607\) CPM, and the average coverage doubles to 0.986, so RPGC 243. Both remove the effect of depth. The ratio RPGC / CPM = genome size / (fragment length × \(10^6\)) \(= 40\,088\,619 / 10^8 = 0.40\), the same for every position (C7b).

B6. (a) A: 60/12 = 5, log2(61/13) = 2.23. B: 3/0 = ∞, log2(4/1) = 2.0. C: 0/2 = 0, log2(1/3) = −1.58. D: 0/0 undefined, log2(1/1) = 0. (b) No. Bin A rests on 72 reads, bin B on 3: one read more or less changes B’s ratio a lot (2 reads would give 1.58, 4 reads 2.32). Use larger bins, a deeper or smoothed input (MACS-style local background over 1–10 kb), or require a minimum count. The pseudocount already pulls B down from ∞ to 2.

B7. (a) r2 and r5 are duplicates of r1 (same 5’ end 1000, + strand). r7 duplicates r6 (− strand; both have 5’ end 1 245). r4 starts 3 bp later: not a duplicate. Three duplicates out of 8: 37.5% (markdup keeps the copy with the best qualities and flags the others). (b) r3 is on the − strand: its 5’ end is at 1 035 and it came from a different fragment, extending to the left. Duplicates must match position and strand. (c) PCR duplicates are concentrated where the reads are, but there is also a real effect: with single-end 36 bp reads, a ~200 bp peak offers only about 200 possible start positions per strand. With tens of thousands of fragments in a strong peak, independent fragments often share a start by chance (saturation), so dedup also removes real signal at the strongest peaks. With paired-end reads, two fragments must share both ends to be called duplicates, which is much rarer by chance.

B8. (a) Trimming (before alignment, so adapters do not force mismatches) → alignment → sorting (markdup, indexing and region access need positions in order) → MAPQ/FLAG filter (judge duplicates only among reads you trust) → duplicate marking → blacklist removal (positional; can be anywhere after alignment, but before computing totals for normalisation) → bamCoverage (last, so the CPM denominator counts only the reads that are kept). (b) Too low: CPM divides by all reads in the BAM, including the chrM reads. With 66% on chrM, the chr21 signal is divided by about three times too many reads, so it is about 3× too low (1/0.34 = 2.9), and a ChIP/input ratio is about 3× too high. (c) Multi-mapping reads pile up in repeats (signal where it cannot be trusted, and reads randomly placed between copies); duplicates inflate some positions into spikes; blacklisted regions show huge artefact peaks (e.g. 451 input reads at chr21:10.01 Mb); and the CPM denominator includes chrM and unmapped-to-chr21 reads, so all values are scaled differently.

B9. (a) A read is either unique (MAPQ 42 when the second-best alignment is much worse) or has several equally good places (MAPQ 0–1). Intermediate values need a second-best alignment that is a little worse, which is rarer. So any threshold between about 5 and 30 removes almost the same reads. (b) About 50 + 50 = 100 bp (the medians are −50 and +48). The protein occupies the DNA around the motif: fragment ends cannot fall where CTCF sits, so there is a footprint of about ±18 bp with almost no 5’ ends. (c) No information is not evidence of absence: with MAPQ ≥ 30, reads in a repeat with several identical copies are removed, so the region is blind. To ask about binding there, you need longer or paired reads, multi-mapper-aware methods, or a different assay.

Where this leads

  • Experimental design and QC turns the numbers in your C5 table into QC metrics: library complexity from duplicates, FRiP (R4b), fingerprints and strand cross-correlation.
  • ChIP-seq peak calling starts from chip.final.bam and input.final.bam and replaces the noisy per-bin ratio with MACS3’s local Poisson background.
  • ATAC-seq processing and peak calling uses the same chain with paired-end reads, adapter trimming that really matters, chrM removal and the Tn5 shift.
  • The production version of all of this is nf-core/chipseq or nf-core/atacseq.
