Exercise: Calling compartments, TADs and loops

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Compute observed/expected values, find the compartment pattern and choose the sign of the eigenvector, on a toy matrix by hand.
  • Compute an insulation score and an APA score by hand, and decide whether a bright pixel is a loop.
  • Call compartments, boundaries and dots on real GM12878 Hi-C with cooltools, and test them against GC content, histone marks, CTCF ChIP-seq and published calls.
  • Find the orientation of CTCF motifs at loop anchors in R, and judge claims about differential TADs and loops.

Background

Observed/expected. \(E(s)\) is the mean balanced contact frequency of all pixels at distance \(s\) (one diagonal); O/E = pixel / \(E(s)\).

Compartments. Correlate the rows of the O/E matrix (or centre it) and take the first eigenvector, E1. Bins with the same sign have similar contact profiles. The sign is arbitrary: flip E1 so that it is positive where GC content (or gene density) is high: that is compartment A. A saddle plot averages O/E over pairs of E1 groups; strength = (AA + BB)/(AB + BA).

Insulation. For each position, sum the contacts in a \(w \times w\) window that straddles it, just off the diagonal; the score is \(\log_2\)(sum / mean sum along the chromosome). Boundaries are local minima.

Dots. A loop pixel is tested against a local expected value: its distance-expected \(E\) scaled by observed/expected in a neighbourhood (donut, lower-left, horizontal, vertical), with a Poisson test. APA averages O/E submatrices around many loops; score = centre / mean of the lower-left corner.

You need cooler, cooltools, samtools and bedtools on the command line; in Python cooler, cooltools, bioframe, pyfaidx, pyBigWig, numpy, pandas and matplotlib (one conda line: conda create -n hic -c conda-forge -c bioconda python=3.11 cooler=0.10 cooltools=0.7 pairtools=1.1 bwa samtools bedtools pybigwig pyfaidx bioframe matplotlib); in R GenomicRanges, rtracklayer, Rsamtools, JASPAR2024, TFBSTools and motifmatchr, as in Gene regulation. The whole exercise runs in about 10 minutes on a laptop; cooltools dots takes 1–3 minutes.

Simplification: we analyse two chromosomes of a published, very deep map, at one or a few resolutions. Calls on a whole genome, on shallower data, or with other callers will differ; that is part of the lesson.

By hand

B1. Compartments on a toy chromosome. A balanced map of six 1-Mb bins (upper triangle):

        1     2     3     4     5     6
1       –    10    15     9     2     2
2             –    10     5    10     6
3                   –    30     5     3
4                         –    10     5
5                               –    30
6                                     –

  1. Compute the expected value \(E(s)\) for distances of 1, 2 and 3 bins (the mean of each diagonal).

  2. Compute O/E for pixels (1, 3), (2, 4), (1, 4), (2, 5) and (3, 6). Which bins seem to belong together?

  3. Sort the six bins into two groups by eye from the O/E values.

  4. An eigensolver returns E1 = (−0.41, 0.40, −0.42, −0.40, 0.41, 0.42). GC content of the bins is 0.48, 0.38, 0.50, 0.47, 0.40, 0.37. Which bins are compartment A? What would have happened in a genome-wide analysis if you had not checked the sign?

B2. Insulation by hand. In this 8-bin map, bins 1–4 and 5–8 are two domains. Contacts within a domain are 40, 22, 15 at distances 1, 2, 3 bins; across the boundary they are 8, 5, 4, 3 at distances 1, 2, 3, 4.

  1. With a window of \(w = 2\) bins, the window for the gap after bin \(k\) covers pixels \((k-1, k+1)\), \((k-1, k+2)\), \((k, k+1)\), \((k, k+2)\). Compute the window sums for the gaps after bins 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6.

  2. Compute the insulation score \(\log_2\)(sum / mean of the five sums) at each gap. Where is the boundary, and how deep is it?

  3. What would happen with \(w = 1\), and with \(w = 4\), in a real map with nested domains?

B3. Read a saddle plot. Three E1 groups (B, middle, A), mean O/E:

            control                 cohesin depleted
         B     mid    A           B     mid    A
B       2.0    1.0   0.4         2.4    1.0   0.3
mid     1.0    1.1   0.9         1.0    1.1   0.9
A       0.4    0.9   1.5         0.3    0.9   1.9
  1. Compute compartment strength in both conditions. (b) Is the change what you expect from 3D genome organisation? Why? (c) What must be the same in the two analyses for the comparison to be fair?

B4. Is it a loop? Two pixels are 200 kb from the diagonal, where the distance-expected count is \(E = 40\).

pixel count donut: observed sum donut: expected sum
pixel X 120 400 320
pixel Y 70 1 120 700

  1. Compute O/E against the distance-expected value for each pixel.

  2. Compute the donut-corrected local expected value (\(E \times\) observed sum / expected sum) and O/E against it. Which pixel is a loop candidate?

  3. Pixel Y lies at the corner of a strong domain. Explain why the distance-expected value alone would mislead you.

  4. For pixel X, is a count of 120 surprising if the true mean is the local expected value? (A Poisson variable with mean 50 has standard deviation about 7.)

B5. APA. An APA pileup of 400 loops (O/E, 21 × 21 pixels) has a centre value of 3.2 and a lower-left 6 × 6 mean of 1.4. The same 400 loop positions piled up in a Hi-C map of a different cell type give 1.5 and 1.3.

  1. Compute both APA scores. (b) What do you conclude about these loops in the second cell type? (c) Why use the lower-left corner rather than the whole pileup as background?

B6. CTCF orientation. At ten loops, the strongest CTCF motif at the left and right anchor lies on these strands:

+ −    + −    + +    + −    − −    + −    − +    + −    + −    + −
  1. What fraction is convergent, tandem and divergent? What fractions would you expect if orientations were random? (b) Which model explains the excess of convergent pairs, and why are divergent pairs nearly absent at loop anchors although divergent CTCF pairs are common at domain boundaries?

B7. Judge the claims.

  1. “Knockout cells have 30% fewer loops than wild type (6 900 vs 9 800, HiCCUPS).” The knockout map has 400 M contacts, the wild type 1.2 B, one replicate each.

  2. “TADs are unchanged after the treatment: 95% of boundaries overlap between conditions.” The calls were made at 40-kb resolution with a single-scale caller.

  3. “This enhancer forms a loop with gene X (HiChIP for H3K27ac), so it regulates X.”

B8. Resolution. A Hi-C experiment gives 300 million contacts genome-wide (about 60% within chromosomes). Which features can you analyse: compartments, domains, loops? At what bin size? (Rule of thumb from Rao et al. 2014: the resolution is the smallest bin size at which 80% of bins have at least 1 000 contacts.)

for w in 100000 200000 500000; do
  awk -F'\t' -v c="is_boundary_$w" 'NR == 1 { for (i = 1; i <= NF; i++) if ($i == c) k = i; next } $k == "True"' \
    insulation_10kb.tsv | wc -l

In R

R1 — CTCF motif orientation at loop anchors

As in Gene regulation, scan 100 bp around each CTCF peak summit with the JASPAR CTCF matrix, keep the strand of the best match, and give each loop anchor the motif of its strongest CTCF peak:

suppressPackageStartupMessages({
  library(GenomicRanges); library(rtracklayer); library(Rsamtools); library(Biostrings)
  library(JASPAR2024); library(TFBSTools); library(RSQLite); library(motifmatchr)
})
D <- "/path/to/course/data"
lp <- read.table(file.path(D, "gm12878_hic_loops_hiccups.bedpe.gz"), sep = "\t", comment.char = "#")[, 1:6]
names(lp) <- c("chrom1", "start1", "end1", "chrom2", "start2", "end2")
a1 <- GRanges(lp$chrom1, IRanges(lp$start1 + 1, lp$end1))
a2 <- GRanges(lp$chrom2, IRanges(lp$start2 + 1, lp$end2))
ctcf <- import(file.path(D, "gm12878_ctcf_peaks.bed.gz"), format = "narrowPeak")
ctcf <- ctcf[overlapsAny(ctcf, c(a1, a2))]          # only peaks in loop anchors
win <- resize(GRanges(seqnames(ctcf), IRanges(start(ctcf) + ctcf$peak, width = 1)), 100, fix = "center")
ctcf_m <- getMatrixByID(dbConnect(SQLite(), db(JASPAR2024())), "MA0139.2")
pos <- matchMotifs(ctcf_m, getSeq(FaFile(file.path(D, "hg38.fa.bgz")), win), p.cutoff = 1e-4, bg = "even",
                   out = "positions")[[1]]
ctcf$motif <- vapply(pos, function(x) if (length(x) == 0) NA_character_ else
                     as.character(mcols(x)$strand)[which.max(mcols(x)$score)], "")
c(peaks = length(ctcf), with_motif = sum(!is.na(ctcf$motif)))
anchor_motif <- function(a) {
  h <- findOverlaps(a, ctcf)
  d <- data.frame(q = queryHits(h), s = subjectHits(h), sig = ctcf$signalValue[subjectHits(h)])
  d <- d[!is.na(ctcf$motif[d$s]), ]
  d <- d[order(d$q, -d$sig), ]; d <- d[!duplicated(d$q), ]
  out <- rep(NA_character_, length(a)); out[d$q] <- ctcf$motif[d$s]; out
}
lp$motif1 <- anchor_motif(a1); lp$motif2 <- anchor_motif(a2)
write.table(lp, "loop_ctcf_orientation.tsv", sep = "\t", quote = FALSE, row.names = FALSE)

  1. How many CTCF peaks lie in loop anchors, and how many of them have a motif match?

  2. Why take the strongest peak per anchor? What could go wrong with an anchor 25 kb wide?

In Python

import numpy as np, pandas as pd, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import cooler, cooltools, bioframe, pyBigWig, subprocess
D = "/path/to/course/data"
MC = f"{D}/gm12878_hic_chr19_chr21.mcool::resolutions/"
CHROMS = ["chr19", "chr21"]
def view_of(clr):
    return pd.DataFrame({"chrom": CHROMS, "start": 0, "end": [clr.chromsizes[c] for c in CHROMS], "name": CHROMS})
def bw_mean(path, df):
    b = pyBigWig.open(path)
    v = [b.stats(r.chrom, int(r.start), int(min(r.end, b.chroms(r.chrom))), type="mean")[0] for r in df.itertuples()]
    return np.array([np.nan if x is None else x for x in v], dtype=float)

P4 — Dots

exp10 = cooltools.expected_cis(c10, view_df=v10, nproc=4)
dots = cooltools.dots(c10, expected=exp10, view_df=v10, max_loci_separation=2_000_000, nproc=4)
print(len(dots), "dots;", dots.groupby("chrom1").size().to_dict())
hic = pd.read_csv(f"{D}/gm12878_hic_loops_hiccups.bedpe.gz", sep="\t", comment="#", header=None).iloc[:, :6]
hic.columns = ["chrom1", "start1", "end1", "chrom2", "start2", "end2"]
hic = hic[hic.chrom1.isin(CHROMS)]
def frac_matched(a, b, tol=20_000):
    hits = 0
    for r in a.itertuples():
        x = b[b.chrom1 == r.chrom1]
        d1 = np.abs((x.start1 + x.end1) / 2 - (r.start1 + r.end1) / 2)
        d2 = np.abs((x.start2 + x.end2) / 2 - (r.start2 + r.end2) / 2)
        hits += np.any((d1 <= tol) & (d2 <= tol))
    return hits / len(a)
hic2 = hic[hic.start2 - hic.start1 <= 2_000_000]
print("ours in HiCCUPS:", round(frac_matched(dots, hic), 2), " HiCCUPS (<= 2 Mb) in ours:", round(frac_matched(hic2, dots), 2))

  1. How many dots does cooltools find, and how many HiCCUPS loops are on the two chromosomes?

  2. What fraction of our dots is confirmed, and what fraction of HiCCUPS loops do we recover? Give three reasons for the difference.

P5 — APA by CTCF orientation

This uses the table written in R1.

lo = pd.read_csv("loop_ctcf_orientation.tsv", sep="\t")
lo = lo[lo.chrom1.isin(CHROMS)]
lo["cls"] = lo.motif1.fillna("0") + lo.motif2.fillna("0")
def to_feat(t):
    m1, m2 = (t.start1 + t.end1) // 2, (t.start2 + t.end2) // 2
    f = pd.DataFrame({"chrom1": t.chrom1, "start1": m1 - 5000, "end1": m1 + 5000,
                      "chrom2": t.chrom2, "start2": m2 - 5000, "end2": m2 + 5000})
    return f[(f.start2 - f.start1) >= 150_000]
sets = {"convergent": to_feat(lo[lo.cls == "+-"]), "other": to_feat(lo[lo.cls != "+-"])}
shifted = to_feat(lo).copy()
for k in ["start1", "end1", "start2", "end2"]:
    shifted[k] += 500_000
sets["shifted 500 kb"] = shifted[shifted.end2 < shifted.chrom2.map(c10.chromsizes)]
fig, axs = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 4))

Hints

Hint 1 — compartments and saddles (B1, B3, P1, P2)

\(E(s)\) is the mean of one diagonal. Bins in the same compartment have O/E > 1 with each other at every distance. Check the sign of E1 against a track: here, bins with higher GC must get E1 > 0. Strength = (AA + BB) / (AB + BA) from the corners of the saddle. Papers: Lieberman-Aiden et al. 2009; tools: cooltools.eigs_cis, cooltools.saddle, bioframe.frac_gc, and the compartments tutorial.

Hint 2 — insulation (B2, C1–C3, P3)

Write out the four pixels of each window, look them up, add. \(\log_2(x) = \ln x / \ln 2\). The original insulation score: Crane et al. 2015; cooltools.insulation and its tutorial; the cooltools CLI; bedtools window reports features within a distance. Caller comparisons: Forcato et al. 2017.

Hint 3 — dots and APA (B4, B5, P4, P5)

Local expected = \(E \times\) (observed in kernel / expected in kernel). For a Poisson mean \(\lambda\), the standard deviation is \(\sqrt{\lambda}\). APA score = centre / lower-left mean. HiCCUPS and APA: Rao et al. 2014; cooltools.dots and its tutorial; cooltools.pileup and the pileup tutorial.

Hint 4 — CTCF orientation (B6, R1, R2)

“+−” means the left anchor’s motif is on the + strand and the right anchor’s on the − strand: they point towards each other. With random strands each of +−, −+, ++, −− has probability 1/4. The R tools are those of Gene regulation: motifmatchr::matchMotifs with out = "positions" (a list per sequence with start, strand and score), JASPAR2024, GenomicRanges::findOverlaps. Loop extrusion: Fudenberg et al. 2016.

Hint 5 — judging claims (B7, B8)

Ask: equal depth? replicates? which caller, which resolution? is the statistic continuous (insulation, APA) or a binary call? does the assay itself enrich for the feature (HiChIP)? does the claim need a perturbation? For B8, count bins: the genome is 3.1 Gb; each contact adds to the row sums of two bins. Review: Lajoie et al. 2015.

Solutions — by hand

B1. (a) Distance 1: (10 + 10 + 30 + 10 + 30) / 5 = 18. Distance 2: (15 + 5 + 5 + 5) / 4 = 7.5. Distance 3: (9 + 10 + 3) / 3 = 7.33. (b) (1, 3) = 15 / 7.5 = 2.0; (2, 4) = 5 / 7.5 = 0.67; (1, 4) = 9 / 7.33 = 1.23; (2, 5) = 10 / 7.33 = 1.36; (3, 6) = 3 / 7.33 = 0.41. Bins 1, 3, 4 attract each other, and 2, 5, 6 attract each other. (c) {1, 3, 4} and {2, 5, 6}: also (3, 4) = 30 and (5, 6) = 30 are high against 18, while (1, 2), (2, 3), (4, 5) are low. (d) GC is high in bins 1, 3, 4 (0.47–0.50), where E1 is negative: flip the sign. A = bins 1, 3, 4, B = 2, 5, 6. Without the check, chromosomes would be labelled at random: about half of them would have A and B swapped, and genome-wide statistics (e.g. “A is gene-rich”) would be diluted or reversed.

B2. (a) After bin 2: (1, 3) 22 + (1, 4) 15 + (2, 3) 40 + (2, 4) 22 = 99. After 3: (2, 4) 22 + (2, 5) 4 + (3, 4) 40 + (3, 5) 5 = 71. After 4: (3, 5) 5 + (3, 6) 4 + (4, 5) 8 + (4, 6) 5 = 22. After 5: (4, 6) 5 + (4, 7) 4 + (5, 6) 40 + (5, 7) 22 = 71. After 6: (5, 7) 22 + (5, 8) 15 + (6, 7) 40 + (6, 8) 22 = 99. (b) Mean = 362 / 5 = 72.4. Scores: log2(99/72.4) = +0.45, log2(71/72.4) = −0.03, log2(22/72.4) = −1.72, −0.03, +0.45. The boundary is between bins 4 and 5, a dip of about 1.7 below its neighbours (2.2 in log2 units against the flanks at +0.45). (c) \(w = 1\) uses one pixel per position: noisy, and it finds sub-domain boundaries; \(w = 4\) (as large as the domains) smooths over small domains and only shows the main boundaries; with nested domains each window size gives a different, valid set (the lecture’s 261 / 160 / 98 boundaries).

B3. (a) Control: (2.0 + 1.5) / (0.4 + 0.4) = 4.4. Cohesin depleted: (2.4 + 1.9) / (0.3 + 0.3) = 7.2. (b) Yes: without cohesin, loop extrusion no longer mixes neighbouring A and B segments, so compartmentalisation strengthens (Rao et al. 2017; Schwarzer et al. 2017). (c) The same resolution and E1 groups (ideally E1 from one condition or from both pooled), similar depth, the same distance range (excluding the diagonal), and the same expected-value normalisation for each condition.

B4. (a) X: 120 / 40 = 3.0; Y: 70 / 40 = 1.75. (b) X: local expected = 40 × 400/320 = 50; O/E = 120/50 = 2.4. Y: local expected = 40 × 1 120/700 = 64; O/E = 70/64 = 1.09. X is a loop candidate; Y is not. (c) Y sits in a region (a domain) where every pixel is enriched, so it is above the distance-expected value but not above its neighbours: a domain corner without a dot. (d) Very: 120 is (120 − 50)/7 ≈ 10 standard deviations above the mean; the Poisson p-value is far below any threshold, even after correcting for millions of tests.

B5. (a) 3.2 / 1.4 = 2.3 in the first cell type; 1.5 / 1.3 = 1.15 in the second. (b) The loops are much weaker or absent in the second cell type on average (or the second map has lower quality or resolution: check its APA at its own loops before concluding). Some may persist: an average hides heterogeneity, so look at subsets or at individual strong loops. (c) The whole pileup includes the stripes and domain signal around the loop; the lower-left corner (towards the diagonal) is the strongest background, so the score is conservative: a high score means a point-like enrichment, not just a domain.

B6. (a) Convergent (+−): 7 of 10 = 70%; tandem (++, −−): 2 = 20%; divergent (−+): 1 = 10%. Random expectation: 25% convergent, 50% tandem, 25% divergent. (b) Loop extrusion: cohesin stops at CTCF bound with its N-terminus facing the loop, i.e. motifs pointing into the loop; so loops form between convergent sites. Divergent sites (← →) point away from each other: each stops cohesin coming from outside, so a divergent pair separates two domains (a strong boundary) rather than anchoring a loop between them.

B7. (a) Not comparable: the knockout map has a third of the depth, and loop calls depend strongly on depth; one replicate gives no variance. Downsample the wild type to 400 M and call again, use replicates, and compare loop strength at a common loop set (APA, or counts at fixed loop positions) rather than numbers of calls. (b) At 40 kb with one scale, boundary positions are coarse and the call is binary: insulation strength can drop by half while the boundary is still “called”. Compare insulation scores or boundary strengths quantitatively at higher resolution, with replicates, and check several window sizes. (c) H3K27ac HiChIP enriches contacts at acetylated anchors, so active elements look connected partly because they are acetylated; and a contact is not regulation. Test with a perturbation: CRISPRi or deletion of the enhancer and measure X (and other genes).

B8. About 180 M cis contacts. Compartments at 100 kb–1 Mb: easily (3.1 Gb / 100 kb = 31 000 bins, about 10 000 contacts per bin). Domains at 25–50 kb: possible (62 000–124 000 bins, a few thousand contacts each). Loops at 5–10 kb need ~1 000+ contacts in 80% of bins: at 10 kb there are 310 000 bins and 600 M row-sum entries (2 × 300 M), about 1 900 per bin on average, so 10 kb is borderline and 5 kb out of reach; Rao et al. needed billions of contacts for 1–5 kb. Use APA at known loops rather than calling loops de novo.

Solutions — R

R1. (a) 15 172 CTCF peaks lie in loop anchors; 12 761 (84%) of them have a motif match at \(p < 10^{-4}\) within 50 bp of the summit. (b) An anchor of 5–25 kb can contain several CTCF peaks, sometimes in opposite orientations; the strongest peak is the most likely to be the one stopping cohesin, but not always. Peaks without a match, or matches in both orientations, add noise; the true fraction of convergent loops is therefore higher than what we measure.

Where this leads

  • Linking regulatory elements to genes uses contact frequencies (and the activity-by-contact model) to assign enhancers to target genes.
  • Epigenomics and genetic variation asks whether disease variants fall in loop anchors and domain boundaries.
  • For a new Hi-C or Micro-C data set, the same steps run genome-wide with the open2c distiller pipeline, or nf-core/hic, followed by cooltools.
