Exercise: Motifs, motif enrichment and TF footprinting

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Compute the expected number of chance motif matches, and fill in and test a 2×2 motif enrichment table (odds ratio, Fisher / hypergeometric test), by hand and in R.
  • Correct for testing hundreds of motifs (Bonferroni, Benjamini–Hochberg) and explain why the background and GC content matter.
  • Find the motifs enriched in B-cell- and T-cell-specific ATAC-seq peaks with JASPAR 2024 and motifmatchr.
  • Compute and read chromVAR deviation z-scores across 13 blood cell types with chromVAR.
  • Align ATAC-seq reads, turn them into Tn5 insertion sites, and plot an aggregate CTCF footprint at bound and unbound motif sites.

Background

Motif matches. A position weight matrix (PWM, from Gene regulation) gives each window of DNA a log-odds score; a window is a match when its score would be reached by chance with probability below a threshold \(p\) (motifmatchr uses \(p < 5 \times 10^{-5}\)). A region of length \(L\) has about \(2L\) windows (both strands), so it contains on average \(2Lp\) chance matches.

Enrichment. For a target set of peaks (for example B-up) and a background set (the other tested peaks):

with match without
target \(a\) \(b\)
background \(c\) \(d\)

Odds ratio \(\mathrm{OR} = ad / bc\). Under the null, the target is a random draw of \(a + b\) peaks from all \(N = a + b + c + d\) peaks, of which \(K = a + c\) have a match; the expected number in the target is \((a+b)K/N\). The one-sided p-value is the hypergeometric upper tail \(P(X \ge a)\), which equals the one-sided Fisher’s exact test.

Multiple testing. With \(m\) motifs, Bonferroni rejects when \(p < 0.05/m\). Benjamini–Hochberg (BH): sort the p-values, find the largest \(i\) with \(p_{(i)} \le 0.05\, i/m\), and reject the \(i\) smallest.

chromVAR computes, for each motif and sample, the fraction of reads in peaks with the motif, compares it with the expected fraction (all samples pooled), and standardises it against background peak sets matched for GC content and average accessibility: a deviation z-score. Variability is the standard deviation of a motif’s z-scores across samples.

Footprints. Each read end marks one Tn5 insertion: read start + 4 on the + strand, read end − 5 on the − strand. Averaging insertions around many motif sites, oriented by motif strand, gives an aggregate footprint: a dip where the bound protein blocks Tn5, flanked by high insertion rates in the open DNA next to it.

Data

  • Corces et al. 2016 haematopoiesis ATAC-seq (GEO GSE74912): read counts in 590 650 fixed 500-bp peaks (hg19) for 132 samples. The 77 samples from healthy donors cover 13 cell types; the sample names encode donor and cell type (decoded below from the GEO sample table). The same file as in Differential accessibility, with the UCSC hg19 → hg38 chain file.
  • hg38 genome as a bgzip-compressed, indexed FASTA (hg38.fa.bgz, .fai, .gzi), so R can read any region quickly.
  • GM12878 ATAC-seq, ENCODE ENCSR095QNB replicate 1, paired-end 2 × 100: the 6.1 million read pairs that ENCODE aligned to chr21 or chrM, as raw FASTQ, and a bowtie2 index of chr21 + chrM. Plus the ENCODE GM12878 ATAC peaks and CTCF ChIP-seq peaks (from Genomic intervals).

Shortcuts, said plainly. The footprint uses one chromosome (chr21) of one replicate: aggregate footprints work at this depth, single-site footprints do not. chromVAR uses the 100 000 most-read peaks rather than all 589 000, and sums technical replicates per donor. Motif enrichment is done in R with motifmatchr; HOMER and the MEME suite, the usual command-line tools, are shown in the lecture and under Where this leads but not installed in this course.

You need fastp, bowtie2 and samtools (≥ 1.10) on the command line, and the R packages listed in Install the software you need, including JASPAR2024, TFBSTools, motifmatchr, chromVAR and universalmotif. Total running time on a laptop with 4–8 cores: about 25 minutes, most of it alignment, motif matching and DESeq2.

By hand

Do these on paper.

B1. Chance matches. You scan 300-bp peaks with a motif at threshold \(p = 10^{-4}\).

  1. How many windows does one peak have (both strands, ignore the ends)? How many chance matches do you expect per peak?

  2. In 50 000 peaks, how many chance matches do you expect in total?

  3. Chance matches are rare events, so the number per peak is roughly Poisson. What fraction of peaks has at least one chance match? (\(P(\ge 1) = 1 - e^{-\lambda}\).)

  4. A paper reports “a GATA motif in 25% of our peaks”. What do you need to know before this means anything?

B2. A 2×2 table. 400 target peaks, 120 with a match to motif M. 9600 background peaks, 1440 with a match.

  1. Write the 2×2 table and the fraction with a match in each group.

  2. Compute the odds ratio.

  3. How many target peaks would you expect to have a match if the target were a random draw from all 10 000 peaks? Is the observation higher or lower?

  4. In the real data, an EBF1 match is in 2724 of 19 439 B-up peaks and in 23 440 of 192 385 other tested peaks; \(p \approx 4 \times 10^{-13}\). Compute the two fractions and the odds ratio. Is EBF1 “the TF that drives B-cell chromatin”? What else would you look at?

B3. Many motifs. You test 800 motifs. The eight smallest p-values are

\(1\times10^{-40}\), \(3\times10^{-12}\), \(2\times10^{-6}\), \(4\times10^{-5}\), \(9\times10^{-5}\), \(1.5\times10^{-4}\), \(2.5\times10^{-4}\), \(6\times10^{-4}\).

  1. What is the Bonferroni threshold at 5%, and how many of these motifs pass?

  2. Apply Benjamini–Hochberg at FDR 5%. How many pass?

  3. If none of the 800 motifs were truly enriched, how many would you expect below \(p = 0.05\)?

  4. The top 30 motifs include 12 ETS motifs (ELF1, ELF3, ETV1, GABPA, …). How many independent findings is that?

B4. Background and GC. Your target peaks are promoters (mean GC 0.62). You compare them with (i) random 500-bp windows of the genome (mean GC 0.41) and (ii) GC-matched peaks from the same cells.

  1. With background (i), which kinds of motifs will look enriched only because of base composition? Give an example.

  2. Why does the comparison with (ii) answer a more useful question?

  3. In the real B-up peaks (mean GC 0.48, other peaks 0.51), the SP1 motif has OR 0.60 against the other peaks and 0.93 against GC-matched peaks. Which number would you report, and what do you conclude about SP1?

B5. One motif, many TFs. In B-up peaks the three most enriched motifs are SPIB (OR 2.87), SPI1 (PU.1, OR 2.58) and SPIC (OR 2.80).

  1. Can you tell from these numbers which of the three TFs binds the B-cell peaks? Why not?

  2. Name two kinds of data that would help.

  3. In T-up peaks, ETS1 has OR 2.67 but SPI1 only 1.18. How can two ETS motifs behave so differently? (Hint: look at the logos in the lecture.)

B6. Read a chromVAR heatmap. Part of a deviation z-score table (mean over donors):

motif HSC MEP Mono B CD4 T
GATA1::TAL1 1 66 −22 −21 −24
SPI1 3 −15 22 21 −12
CEBPA −1 −5 25 −4 −5
TCF7 −5 −2 −5 0 13

  1. For each motif, name the cell types where peaks with the motif are more accessible than expected.

  2. What does a z-score of −22 for GATA1::TAL1 in monocytes mean? Does it mean GATA1 represses those peaks in monocytes?

  3. chromVAR reports a motif named “GATA3” with almost the same z-scores as GATA1::TAL1, highest in MEPs and erythroblasts, although GATA3 is a T-cell factor. Explain.

  4. Why does chromVAR compare with background peaks of matched GC content and accessibility instead of just using the raw deviation?

B7. Read an aggregate footprint. Mean Tn5 insertions per site around 1000 CTCF motif sites inside CTCF ChIP peaks: about 0.70 at ±30–60 bp from the motif centre, 0.25 within ±10 bp of the centre, and 0.30 at ±100 bp.

  1. Sketch the profile. Which features are the flanks and which is the footprint?

  2. Compute the ratio centre/flank. What would it be for a motif without any protection?

  3. For 5000 CTCF motif sites in closed chromatin the profile is flat at 0.01. Why flat, and why so low?

  4. A colleague finds no footprint for the glucocorticoid receptor at its ChIP-seq peaks and concludes that it does not bind there. What is wrong with this conclusion?

  5. An aggregate footprint of motif X shows a sharp 3-bp dip at the same position in cells with and without the TF. What is the likely explanation, and how would you correct for it?

B8. Judge the claim. “Peaks gained after stimulation are enriched for AP-1 motifs (p < 10⁻¹⁰⁰ against random genomic regions), so AP-1 opens these regions.” List three problems or missing controls.

fastp -i $D/gm12878_atac_rep1_R1.fastq.gz -I $D/gm12878_atac_rep1_R2.fastq.gz \

  | samtools fixmate -m -u - - \

R1 — The differential peaks (given)

This block reproduces the B-cell vs CD4 T-cell analysis of Differential accessibility in compact form: decode the sample names, lift the peaks to hg38, sum technical replicates, and run DESeq2 with ~ donor + celltype on the three donors that have both cell types. Run it; it takes 2–3 minutes.

cnt <- read.delim(file.path(D, "corces2016_atac_counts.txt.gz"), check.names = FALSE)
peaks19 <- GRanges(cnt$Chr, IRanges(cnt$Start + 1, cnt$End))        # BED-like 0-based starts
cnt <- as.matrix(cnt[, -(1:3)])

# sample names -> donor and cell type (codes from the GEO sample table, GSE74912)
code <- c("1" = "HSC", "2" = "MPP", "3" = "LMPP", "4" = "CMP", "5" = "GMP", "6" = "MEP",
          "7" = "Mono", "9" = "CD4", "10" = "CD8", "11" = "NK", "13" = "B", "14" = "CLP",
          "15" = "Ery")
nm <- colnames(cnt)
ok <- grepl("^(Donor)?[0-9]{4}-", nm)                                # healthy donors only
info <- data.frame(sample = nm[ok],
                   donor = sub("^(Donor)?([0-9]{4})-.*", "\\2", nm[ok]),
                   celltype = code[sub(".*-([0-9]{1,2})[A-C]?(-[0-9]{6})?$", "\\1", nm[ok])])
table(info$celltype)

# lift to hg38; keep peaks that map in one piece of 500 bp
chain <- tempfile(fileext = ".chain")
writeLines(readLines(file.path(D, "hg19ToHg38.over.chain.gz")), chain)
lo <- liftOver(peaks19, import.chain(chain))
keep <- lengths(lo) == 1 & sum(width(lo)) == 500
peaks <- unlist(lo[keep]); cnt <- cnt[keep, info$sample]

# B vs CD4 T in donors with both; technical replicates (A/B) summed
bt <- info[info$celltype %in% c("B", "CD4"), ]
bt <- bt[bt$donor %in% intersect(bt$donor[bt$celltype == "B"], bt$donor[bt$celltype == "CD4"]), ]
m <- sapply(split(bt$sample, paste(bt$donor, bt$celltype, sep = "_")),
            function(s) rowSums(cnt[, s, drop = FALSE]))
cd <- data.frame(donor = factor(sub("_.*", "", colnames(m))),
                 celltype = factor(sub(".*_", "", colnames(m)), levels = c("CD4", "B")))
use <- rowSums(m >= 10) >= 3                                         # drop barely detected peaks
dds <- DESeq(DESeqDataSetFromMatrix(m[use, ], cd, ~ donor + celltype), quiet = TRUE)
res <- results(dds, name = "celltype_B_vs_CD4")
tested <- peaks[use]; tested$lfc <- res$log2FoldChange; tested$padj <- res$padj
b_up <- !is.na(tested$padj) & tested$padj < 0.05 & tested$lfc > 1
t_up <- !is.na(tested$padj) & tested$padj < 0.05 & tested$lfc < -1
c(hg19 = length(peaks19), hg38 = length(peaks), tested = length(tested),
  B_up = sum(b_up), T_up = sum(t_up))

  1. How many samples does each cell type have? How many peaks were lost in the liftOver?

  2. How many peaks were tested, and how many are B-up and T-up?

R4 — Enrichment in B-up and T-up peaks

First one motif with fisher.test, then all motifs at once with the hypergeometric distribution (identical to one-sided Fisher, but vectorised and on the log scale, so that p-values below \(10^{-308}\) do not become 0):

tab <- table(B_up = b_up, SPIB = hit[, "SPIB_MA0081.3"])
tab
fisher.test(tab, alternative = "greater")

enrich <- function(target, hit, bg = !target) {
  a <- colSums(hit[target, ]); b <- sum(target) - a
  c <- colSums(hit[bg, ]);     d <- sum(bg) - c
  lp <- phyper(a - 1, a + c, b + d, a + b, lower.tail = FALSE, log.p = TRUE) / log(10)
  data.frame(tf = sub("_MA.*", "", colnames(hit)), family = fam[colnames(hit)],
             frac_target = a / (a + b), frac_bg = c / (c + d), OR = (a * d) / (b * c),
             log10p = lp, padj = p.adjust(10^lp, "BH"))
}
eB <- enrich(b_up, hit); eT <- enrich(t_up, hit)
head(eB[order(eB$log10p), -2], 15)
head(eT[order(eT$log10p), -2], 15)
c(B = sum(eB$padj < 0.05 & eB$OR > 1), T = sum(eT$padj < 0.05 & eT$OR > 1))
sel <- c("EBF1_MA0154.5", "PAX5_MA0014.4", "PAX2_MA0067.3", "POU2F2_MA0507.3", "Runx1_MA0002.3",
         "TCF7_MA0769.3", "Gata3_MA0037.5", "ETS1_MA0098.4", "Spi1_MA0080.7", "CTCF_MA0139.2")
round(cbind(B = eB[sel, c("OR", "log10p")], T = eT[sel, c("OR", "log10p")]), 2)

  1. From tab, compute the odds ratio by hand and compare with fisher.test. Why does fisher.test report \(p < 2.2 \times 10^{-16}\) while enrich() gives \(10^{-934}\)?

  2. Which TF families dominate the top of each list? Do they match what you know about B and T cells?

  3. How many motifs are significant at FDR 5%? Is that a useful number?

  4. Look at EBF1, PAX5 and PAX2 in B-up peaks. What do you conclude about the B-cell master regulators EBF1 and PAX5, and about the PAX5 matrix?

  5. CTCF has OR < 1 in both lists. Explain.

R7 — Tn5 insertions and a CTCF footprint

ga <- readGAlignments("gm12878_atac_rep1.bam",
                      param = ScanBamParam(which = GRanges("chr21", IRanges(1, 46709983))))
ins <- GRanges("chr21", IRanges(ifelse(strand(ga) == "+", start(ga) + 4, end(ga) - 5), width = 1),
               seqinfo = Seqinfo("chr21", 46709983))
length(ins)

Hints

Hint 1 — chance matches and the 2×2 table (B1, B2, R3, R4)

Windows per peak ≈ \(2L\); expected matches \(\lambda = 2Lp\); \(P(\ge 1) = 1 - e^{-\lambda}\). Odds ratio \(ad/bc\); expected target matches \((a+b)(a+c)/N\). R: fisher.test, phyper with lower.tail = FALSE gives \(P(X > q)\), so use \(q = a - 1\) for \(P(X \ge a)\). matchMotifs returns a sparse peaks × motifs matrix via motifMatches().

Hint 2 — multiple testing (B3)

Bonferroni: \(p < \alpha / m\). BH: compare \(p_{(i)}\) with \(\alpha\, i / m\) and find the largest \(i\) that passes; all smaller p-values pass too. R: p.adjust.

Hint 3 — motif databases (R2, B5)

JASPAR 2024 (paper: Rauluseviciute et al. 2024); in R the JASPAR2024 package with TFBSTools::getMatrixSet. The family and class of each matrix are in tags(pfm). universalmotif::view_motifs draws and aligns logos. Other collections: CIS-BP, HOCOMOCO.

Hint 4 — chromVAR (B6, R6)

The chromVAR vignette explains addGCBias, getBackgroundPeaks, computeDeviations, deviationScores (z) and computeVariability; method in Schep et al. 2017. A negative z means the motif’s peaks are less accessible than expected in that sample, relative to matched background peaks; it says nothing about the mechanism.

Hint 5 — insertions and footprints (B7, R7, R8)

readGAlignments reads the BAM; start()/end() are the leftmost/rightmost aligned base, so the 5’ end of a − strand read is its end(). coverage of 1-bp ranges counts insertions per base; indexing an Rle with a GRanges and as.matrix gives one row per window. promoters builds strand-aware windows. Residence time and footprints: Sung et al. 2014; bias correction: TOBIAS (Bentsen et al. 2020).

Hint 6 — alignment (C1)

fastp detects and trims the Nextera adapters; bowtie2 -X 2000 allows fragments up to 2 kb; samtools fixmate -m adds the mate tags that samtools markdup needs; samtools idxstats gives reads per chromosome.

Solutions — by hand

B1. (a) About \(2 \times 300 = 600\) windows; \(\lambda = 600 \times 10^{-4} = 0.06\) chance matches per peak. (b) \(50\,000 \times 0.06 = 3000\). (c) \(1 - e^{-0.06} = 0.058\): about 6% of peaks contain a chance match. (d) What fraction of peaks would contain a GATA match by chance (the GATA motif is short, so at a lenient threshold this can be 10–20%), the match threshold and peak width, and above all a comparison with a background set of peaks. “25%” alone is not evidence of anything.

B2. (a)

  1. \(\mathrm{OR} = (120 \times 8160)/(280 \times 1440) = 979\,200 / 403\,200 = 2.43\). (c) \(400 \times 1560 / 10\,000 = 62.4\) expected; 120 observed, about twice as many. (d) \(2724 / 19\,439 = 14.0\%\) vs \(23\,440 / 192\,385 = 12.2\%\); \(\mathrm{OR} = (2724 \times 168\,945)/(16\,715 \times 23\,440) = 1.17\). Highly significant because the sets are large, but a weak effect: on this evidence EBF1 is not a major distinguishing motif. Before concluding, check a GC-matched background (EBF1’s motif is GC-rich and B-up peaks are AT-rich; with GC matching the OR rises to about 1.5, R5), the quality of the matrix, EBF1 expression, EBF1 ChIP-seq, and chromVAR (R6: the EBF1 motif is most accessible in lymphoid progenitors). EBF1 is in fact a B-cell master regulator; motif enrichment just measures it poorly here.

B3. (a) \(0.05 / 800 = 6.25 \times 10^{-5}\): four motifs pass (\(10^{-40}\), \(3 \times 10^{-12}\), \(2 \times 10^{-6}\), \(4 \times 10^{-5}\)). (b) BH thresholds \(0.05\,i/800 = 6.25 \times 10^{-5}\, i\): \(6.25 \times 10^{-5}\), \(1.25 \times 10^{-4}\), \(1.9 \times 10^{-4}\), \(2.5 \times 10^{-4}\), \(3.1 \times 10^{-4}\), \(3.75 \times 10^{-4}\), \(4.4 \times 10^{-4}\), \(5 \times 10^{-4}\). The 7th p-value (\(2.5 \times 10^{-4} \le 4.4 \times 10^{-4}\)) passes, the 8th (\(6 \times 10^{-4} > 5 \times 10^{-4}\)) does not: 7 motifs. (c) \(800 \times 0.05 = 40\). (d) Essentially one: all ETS motifs share the GGAA core, so they are 12 correlated tests of one signal (perhaps two, if PU.1-type and ETS1-type sites separate).

B4. (a) GC-rich and CpG-containing motifs: SP/KLF (GGGCGG), NRF1, E2F, ETS motifs typical of promoters (GABPA), CTCF. They are enriched in promoters against AT-rich random DNA because of composition and because promoters are open, not because they explain anything specific. (b) GC-matched peaks are also open chromatin with the same base composition, so a motif that remains enriched is more frequent in the target than composition and openness alone predict. (c) Report 0.93 (and say which background). The apparent depletion of SP1 against all peaks comes from the B-up peaks being AT-richer (distal elements) than the background, which contains many GC-rich promoters; with GC matched, SP1 is neither enriched nor depleted: no evidence for or against SP1.

B5. (a) No. SPIB, PU.1 and SPIC have nearly identical motifs (one family, one DNA-binding domain); the small differences in OR reflect the matrices, not which protein binds. (b) Expression (RNA-seq of B and T cells: PU.1 and SPIB are expressed in B cells; SPIC mainly in red-pulp macrophages), ChIP-seq or CUT&RUN for each candidate, and perturbation (knock-out or degradation followed by ATAC-seq). (c) The ETS core GGAA is shared, but the flanks differ: PU.1/SPIB prefer an A-rich 5’ flank (AAAGAGGAAGT), ETS1/GABPA prefer CCGGAAGT. T-cell-specific peaks contain ETS1-type sites, B-cell-specific peaks PU.1-type sites, and each matrix detects mostly its own subtype.

B6. (a) GATA1::TAL1: MEPs (and erythroblasts); SPI1: monocytes and B cells; CEBPA: monocytes; TCF7: CD4 T cells. (b) In monocytes, reads in peaks with a GATA motif are much less frequent than expected from the average sample and from matched background peaks: those peaks are relatively closed. It says nothing about repression: most likely no GATA factor is active in monocytes, and the peaks are open only in other cell types. (c) GATA1 and GATA3 bind the same WGATAR motif. chromVAR measures the motif, not the protein; in erythroid cells the GATA-motif peaks are open because of GATA1, whatever name the matrix carries. (d) Samples differ technically (GC bias of the library, fraction of reads in peaks); these shift all peaks with a given GC content or accessibility, motif or not. Background peaks matched on these properties move the same way, so the z-score keeps only the motif-specific part.

B7. (a) Two flanking maxima (≈ 0.7 at ±30–60 bp), a dip over the motif (≈ 0.25, the footprint), lower values further out (≈ 0.3 at ±100 bp). (b) \(0.25 / 0.70 \approx 0.36\). Without protection about 1 (a flat profile, apart from sequence bias). (c) Closed chromatin is inaccessible to Tn5 along the whole window, so there are almost no insertions (0.01 per base per site) and nothing to protect. No open region, no flanks, no dip. (d) The glucocorticoid receptor has a short residence time (about 10 s): it binds and leaves many times during tagmentation, so Tn5 mostly finds the site free. No footprint even though ChIP shows binding (Sung et al. 2014). (e) Tn5 sequence bias at the motif sequence: it is present whether or not the TF is there. Correct with a bias model (TOBIAS ATACorrect, chromBPNet), compare with naked-DNA Tn5 data, or compare bound with unbound sites of the same motif.

B8. (1) The background: random genomic regions are closed and AT-rich, so any set of open regions is enriched for the motifs of open chromatin; AP-1 is the classic motif of active enhancers. Compare with peaks that did not change (GC-matched). (2) The p-value: with thousands of peaks any small difference is significant; report the odds ratio and the fractions. (3) Causality and identity: enrichment is a correlation; the AP-1 family (FOS, JUN, ATF, BATF, …) has many members; to show that AP-1 opens the regions, measure binding (ChIP/CUT&RUN for the stimulated factor) and perturb it (inhibitor, knock-out) to see whether the peaks fail to open. chromVAR or footprinting changes are supportive, not proof.

Where this leads

  • HOMER and MEME do known and de novo motif enrichment from the command line: findMotifsGenome.pl b_up.bed hg38.fa out/ -size 200 -bg tested.bed, or sea --p b_up.fa --n tested.fa --m JASPAR2024.meme and streme (see the lecture).
  • TOBIAS corrects Tn5 bias and compares footprints between conditions: TOBIAS ATACorrect --bam atac.bam --genome hg38.fa --peaks peaks.bed, then ScoreBigwig and BINDetect --motifs JASPAR.meme.
  • chromVAR on single cells (ArchR, Signac) gives each cell a motif z-score; the same idea as R6 with cells as columns.
  • ChIP-seq, CUT&RUN and CUT&Tag measure the binding of one TF directly and give the ground truth that motifs and footprints only suggest.
  • How a CNN reads DNA shows that a convolutional filter is a motif scanner, and models like chromBPNet learn motifs and Tn5 bias from the sequence.
