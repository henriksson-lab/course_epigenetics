Exercise: ChIP-seq: the assay

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Compute DNA amounts, ChIP efficiency and a ChIP-qPCR “percent of input” on paper, and predict the shape of a ChIP-seq track from the target.
  • Process a transcription-factor ChIP (CTCF) and a broad histone-mark ChIP (H3K36me3) with their inputs, reusing the chain from From reads to coverage as a shell function.
  • Compare sharp and broad signals with MACS3 predictd, deepTools plotFingerprint, strand cross-correlation and autocorrelation in R.
  • Show with your own reads that an input is not flat (open promoters) and that H3K36me3 covers transcribed gene bodies.

Background

The protocol. Formaldehyde crosslinks proteins to DNA (and to each other) within ~2 Å; chromatin is fragmented by sonication (crosslinked ChIP) or micrococcal nuclease (native ChIP of histones); an antibody on protein A/G beads pulls down the target; crosslinks are reversed at 65 °C, the DNA is purified (typically 1–10 ng), made into a library and sequenced. The input is the same chromatin taken through every step except the immunoprecipitation.

Amounts. A diploid human cell has about 6.6 pg of DNA. One base pair of double-stranded DNA weighs about 650 g/mol (Avogadro’s number \(6.022 \times 10^{23}\) /mol).

ChIP-qPCR, percent of input. If a fraction \(f\) of the chromatin was kept as input, first adjust its Ct: \(Ct_\text{input,adj} = Ct_\text{input} - \log_2(1/f)\). Then \(\%\text{input} = 100 \times 2^{\,Ct_\text{input,adj} - Ct_\text{ChIP}}\). Fold enrichment between two regions is \(2^{\Delta Ct}\).

Signal shapes. A transcription factor gives sharp peaks about two fragment lengths wide, with + strand reads to the left of the site and − strand reads to the right (separated by the fragment length \(d\)). Histone marks give nucleosome-scale peaks (H3K4me3, H3K27ac: 1–5 kb, with a dip at the nucleosome-free TSS) or broad domains (H3K36me3 over transcribed gene bodies; H3K27me3, H3K9me3 over silent regions, tens to hundreds of kb).

By hand

B1. How much DNA, and how much of it is signal? A CTCF ChIP starts from \(5 \times 10^6\) cells. 1% of the sheared chromatin is kept as input. The IP returns 4 ng of DNA with fragments of about 200 bp.

  1. How much DNA is in the chromatin? How much in the input aliquot?

  2. What fraction of the chromatin DNA did the IP recover?

  3. Suppose 20% of the IP DNA comes from the 40 000 CTCF sites of the genome. How many 200-bp fragments is that? Each site exists in two copies per cell: what fraction of all site copies in the sample ended up in the IP?

  4. The library is sequenced to 20 M reads with FRiP 0.2. On average, how many reads per CTCF site? Compare with (c): what does sequencing do to the library?

B2. ChIP-qPCR. 1% of the chromatin was kept as input. Ct values: input 25.0 (primer pair at site X); ChIP at site X 27.4; ChIP at a negative control region 32.6 (assume the input Ct is the same for both primer pairs).

  1. Compute the percent of input at site X and at the negative region.

  2. What is the fold enrichment of site X over the negative region?

  3. A colleague reports “0.19% of input” as weak. Is it? What would you compare it with?

B3. Strands and resolution.

  1. Around 300 summits of a TF, + strand read 5’ ends peak at −58 bp and − strand 5’ ends at +62 bp. Estimate the fragment length. Why is there no read 5’ end exactly at the summit?

  2. In a ChIP-exo experiment for the same factor, + strand read starts pile up at −12 bp and − strand starts at +14 bp. What do these positions mean, and how large is the protected region?

  3. Why do the same two calculations fail for H3K36me3?

B4. Which antibody? Four ChIP-seq tracks of the same cells, over a 400-kb region with two active genes, one silent developmental gene cluster and an intergenic stretch:

  • Track A: about 15 needle-like peaks, 300–500 bp wide, in genes and between them.
  • Track B: 2–4 kb peaks at the TSSs of the two active genes, each with a small dip at the TSS itself.
  • Track C: low, 30–60 kb “carpets” over the bodies of the two active genes, starting about 1 kb after the TSS and highest towards the 3’ end.
  • Track D: a 150-kb low plateau over the silent gene cluster, nothing over the active genes.

  1. Match each track to one of: H3K27me3, CTCF, H3K36me3, H3K4me3. Give one reason for each.

  2. For which tracks would macs3 predictd find a clear fragment length? Which would need about 45 M reads per replicate by ENCODE’s standards?

  3. Why does track B dip at the TSS?

B5. Read the figures (lecture: Real data: the input is not flat and Real data: profiles around genes).

  1. The input of the CTCF ChIP reaches 14 times the chr21 average at active TSSs, the input of the H3K36me3 ChIP about 1.1 times. Give two reasons why two inputs from the same cell line can differ this much.

  2. A CTCF peak at an active promoter is twice as high as the input there. A CTCF peak in a gene desert is twice as high as the input there too. Which one contains more reads? Are both equally convincing?

  3. Around active TSSs, H3K4me1 is lowest at the TSS and highest about 1 kb away, while H3K4me3 is high on both flanks. What does that tell you about where each mark sits?

B6. Judge the design.

  1. A lab makes a ChIP of a TF in GM12878 and, to save money, uses an input sequenced from K562 cells that was available from ENCODE. What can go wrong?

  2. Another lab sequences the ChIP to 30 M reads and the input to 2 M reads. What happens to a peak caller’s estimate of the local background?

  3. A paper maps a transient co-activator that has no DNA-binding domain with 1% formaldehyde for 5 minutes and finds almost no peaks. Suggest two changes to the protocol.

B7. Hyper-ChIPable? A new antibody against transcription factor Z gives 3 000 peaks. 85% of them lie at the promoters of the 10% most highly expressed genes, and none has the known motif of Z. A ChIP with a non-specific IgG in the same cells gives weaker peaks at many of the same promoters.

  1. What is the most likely explanation?

  2. Name two experiments that would test whether Z binds these promoters.

B8. Crosslinked or native? For each, choose X-ChIP (formaldehyde + sonication) or N-ChIP (no fixation + MNase) and give a reason: (a) H3K27me3 in 50 000 sorted cells; (b) a TF that binds for a few seconds; (c) the positions of H3K4me3 nucleosomes around TSSs, at nucleosome resolution; (d) the co-activator of B6c.

B9. Peak heights. In one CTCF ChIP, site A has 400 reads and site B 100 reads, with similar inputs. A student concludes that CTCF occupies site A in four times as many cells as site B. List three other explanations. What comparison would be meaningful?

C2 — The alignment chain as a function

The chain of From reads to coverage, wrapped in a shell function so that it can be run on any single-end sample:

zcat $D/hg38_blacklist.bed.gz > blacklist.bed
chain() {   # usage: chain <name> <reads.fastq.gz> <33 or 64>
  local s=$1
  if [ "$3" = 64 ]; then zcat $2 | perl -pe 'tr/@-~/!-_/ if $. % 4 == 0' | gzip > $s.fastq.gz
  else cp $2 $s.fastq.gz; fi
  fastp -w $T -i $s.fastq.gz -o $s.trim.fastq.gz -j $s.fastp.json -h $s.fastp.html 2> $s.fastp.log
  bowtie2 -p $T -x $D/bowtie2/hg38_chr21_chrM -U $s.trim.fastq.gz 2> $s.bowtie2.log \
    | samtools sort -@ 4 -o $s.sorted.bam -
  samtools index $s.sorted.bam
  samtools view -b -q 30 -F 1804 -o $s.q30.bam $s.sorted.bam
  samtools markdup -s $s.q30.bam $s.markdup.bam 2> $s.markdup.txt
  samtools index $s.markdup.bam
  samtools view -b -F 1024 -o $s.dedup.bam $s.markdup.bam chr21
  bedtools intersect -v -abam $s.dedup.bam -b blacklist.bed > $s.final.bam
  samtools index $s.final.bam
}
chain ctcf           $D/gm12878_ctcf_chip.fastq.gz      64
chain ctcf_input     $D/gm12878_ctcf_input.fastq.gz     64
chain h3k36me3       $D/gm12878_h3k36me3_chip.fastq.gz  33
chain h3k36me3_input $D/gm12878_h3k36me3_input.fastq.gz 33
for s in ctcf ctcf_input h3k36me3 h3k36me3_input; do
  echo $s $(samtools view -c -F 4 $s.sorted.bam) $(samtools view -c -F 4 $s.sorted.bam chrM) \
    $(grep "DUPLICATE SINGLE:" $s.markdup.txt | cut -d' ' -f3) $(samtools view -c $s.final.bam)
done

  2. The two inputs have very different chrM fractions. Does that matter for a chr21 analysis? (Think about what samtools view ... chr21 does.)

rd <- function(s) granges(readGAlignments(paste0(s, ".final.bam")))
reads <- lapply(c(ctcf = "ctcf", ctcf_input = "ctcf_input", h3k36me3 = "h3k36me3", h3k36me3_input = "h3k36me3_input"), rd)

crosscor <- function(gr, maxk = 500, from = 13e6, to = 46709983) {
  five <- resize(gr, 1, fix = "start")                       # 5' end of each read
  five <- five[start(five) >= from & start(five) <= to - maxk]
  n <- to - from + 1 - maxk
  p <- tabulate(start(five)[as.vector(strand(five) == "+")] - from + 1, n + maxk)
  m <- tabulate(start(five)[as.vector(strand(five) == "-")] - from + 1, n + maxk)
  ip <- which(p[1:n] > 0)
  mp <- mean(p[1:n]); sp <- sd(p[1:n]); mm <- mean(m); sm <- sd(m)
  sapply(0:maxk, function(k) (sum(p[ip] * m[ip + k]) / n - mp * mm) / (sp * sm))
}
cc <- sapply(reads, crosscor)
apply(cc, 2, function(x) c(max = max(x[-(1:41)]), at = which.max(x[-(1:41)]) + 40))   # ignore k < 41
cc[c(1, 37, 52, 101, 201, 301) , ]
par(mfrow = c(1, 2))
matplot(0:500, cc[, 1:2], type = "l", lty = 1, xlab = "shift (bp)", ylab = "cross-correlation", main = "CTCF")
matplot(0:500, cc[, 3:4], type = "l", lty = 1, xlab = "shift (bp)", ylab = "cross-correlation", main = "H3K36me3")

Hints

Hint 1 — amounts and qPCR (B1, B2)

Mass of one fragment = length × 650 g/mol / \(6.022 \times 10^{23}\) /mol. Number of site copies = sites × 2 × cells. For qPCR, an input of 1% is 100-fold diluted relative to the whole chromatin: subtract \(\log_2 100 = 6.64\) cycles. Each cycle doubles: \(\Delta Ct = 1\) is a factor 2. See e.g. the percent-input method.

Hint 2 — strands and shapes (B3, B4, C3, R1)

A single-end read is the 5’ end of a fragment; + strand reads start at the left end of their fragment, − strand reads at the right end. In ChIP-exo the exonuclease stops at the crosslinked protein (Rhee & Pugh 2011). The strand cross-correlation is described in Kharchenko et al. 2008 and in Experimental design and QC; MACS3’s model in the predictd documentation. Where marks sit on genes: Barski et al. 2007.

Hint 3 — controls and artefacts (B5–B7)

Sonication breaks open chromatin more easily; amplified DNA gives more reads everywhere; inputs from different preparations have different biases. Landt et al. 2012 describe ENCODE’s control requirements; Teytelman et al. 2013 and Jain et al. 2015 describe hyper-ChIPable regions and phantom peaks. For the local background of a peak caller, see the MACS λ in Zhang et al. 2008.

Hint 4 — commands (C1–C5)

perl -pe 'tr/@-~/!-_/ if $. % 4 == 0' converts Phred+64 to Phred+33 on every fourth line (From reads to coverage). Shell functions: local keeps a variable inside the function; $1, $2, $3 are its arguments (bash manual). plotFingerprint samples 500 000 bins by default; bamCoverage --extendReads sets the fragment length for single-end reads.

Hint 5 — R (R1–R4)

resize(x, 1, fix = "start") is strand-aware: it gives each read’s 5’ end. countOverlaps only counts features on the same strand unless ignore.strand = TRUE (genes and reads both have a strand). tabulate counts how often each integer occurs; promoters(x, up, down) makes a window around each TSS; mean() of an RleList over a GRanges gives the mean signal per range (rtracklayer import).

Solutions

All numbers come from running the commands as written (fastp 0.23.4, bowtie2 2.4.4, samtools 1.21, bedtools 2.30, MACS3 3.1.0, deepTools 3.5.1, R 4.6 with Bioconductor 3.23).

Solutions — by hand

B1. (a) \(5 \times 10^6 \times 6.6\) pg \(= 33\) µg; the input aliquot 0.33 µg. (b) \(4 \text{ ng} / 33\,000 \text{ ng} = 0.012\%\). (c) One 200-bp fragment weighs \(200 \times 650 / 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 2.2 \times 10^{-19}\) g; 0.8 ng is \(3.7 \times 10^9\) fragments. Site copies: \(40\,000 \times 2 \times 5 \times 10^6 = 4 \times 10^{11}\). So about 0.9% of all site copies were captured: crosslinking, fragmentation, antibody binding and washes each lose most of the material. (d) \(20 \text{ M} \times 0.2 / 40\,000 = 100\) reads per site on average, out of about 93 000 fragments per site in the IP: sequencing samples a tiny fraction of the library (and PCR has made copies of each molecule), which is why duplicates appear only when the number of distinct molecules is small.

B2. (a) \(Ct_\text{input,adj} = 25.0 - 6.64 = 18.36\). Site X: \(100 \times 2^{18.36 - 27.4} = 100 \times 2^{-9.04} = 0.19\%\). Negative region: \(100 \times 2^{18.36 - 32.6} = 0.0052\%\). (b) \(2^{32.6 - 27.4} = 2^{5.2} = 37\)-fold. (c) Not weak: percent input depends on the antibody and the factor, and what matters is the comparison with negative regions (37-fold here) and with a mock IP (IgG). Most of the chromatin at site X is not crosslinked to the factor, or is lost; 0.1–1% is typical for a TF.

B3. (a) \(58 + 62 = 120\) bp. The protein covers the summit: a fragment can only end outside the crosslinked protein, so 5’ ends cannot fall on the site itself, and sonication rarely breaks right next to it. (b) The exonuclease trims each fragment’s 5’ end up to the crosslinked protein: −12 and +14 are the left and right borders of the protected region, about 26 bp, the footprint of the protein (or complex) on the DNA. (c) H3K36me3 has no single site: + and − strand reads both cover the whole domain, so their distributions overlap and the shift between them is not visible; strand peaks cannot be paired (C3, R1).

B4. (a) A = CTCF (sharp, ~2 fragment lengths wide, intergenic as well as genic); B = H3K4me3 (promoter, nucleosome-scale, dip at the nucleosome-free TSS); C = H3K36me3 (gene bodies of transcribed genes, rising to the 3’ end); D = H3K27me3 (broad Polycomb domain over a silent developmental cluster). (b) A clear fragment length: A, and probably B (narrow peaks with strand separation); not C or D. ENCODE’s 45 M reads per replicate apply to broad marks: C and D. (c) The nucleosome-free region at an active TSS has no histones, so a histone mark cannot be there; the signal comes from the −1 and +1 nucleosomes.

B5. (a) Different sonication (more or stronger sonication shears open chromatin preferentially), different fixation, different cell batches and handling (nuclei preparation, chromatin release), different library protocols and read lengths (28 vs 51 bp), and depth. (b) The promoter peak has many more reads (the input there is about 10 times higher than in a gene desert), but both have the same enrichment over their local background. The desert peak is the more convincing evidence of binding per read; the promoter peak would look like binding even with a weak antibody, so it needs the input most. (c) H3K4me3 sits on the nucleosomes immediately flanking the TSS (−1 and +1 and a few downstream); H3K4me1 sits further out, on nucleosomes 1–2 kb away, and is depleted where H3K4me3 is high (the same lysine cannot be both mono- and trimethylated).

B6. (a) K562 has a different karyotype (many amplifications and losses) and different open chromatin: amplified regions of GM12878 are not corrected, K562-specific open regions make GM12878 peaks there look weaker, and GM12878-specific open promoters are uncorrected. The input must come from the same chromatin. (b) With 2 M input reads the 1-kb and even the 10-kb windows contain few reads, so local estimates are noisy; MACS scales the larger library down to the smaller one and the background is often set by the genome-wide λ, which ignores local biases. Sequence the input at least as deep as the ChIP. (c) Crosslink longer (e.g. 10–15 min) and add a protein–protein crosslinker such as DSG before formaldehyde; or use an epitope tag with a validated antibody; check by ChIP-qPCR at expected sites before sequencing.

B7. (a) Hyper-ChIPable regions: highly expressed promoters and genes give ChIP signal with almost any antibody (open chromatin, high protein density, sticky). The lack of the motif and the IgG signal both point that way. (b) ChIP in cells where Z is knocked out or degraded (the peaks should disappear); a second antibody against a different epitope, or an epitope-tagged Z with a tag-only control; check whether the motif of Z is enriched in the remaining peaks after removing those shared with IgG; CUT&RUN with the same antibody (much lower background).

B8. (a) N-ChIP: histone marks survive without fixation and MNase gives clean, low-input libraries (or CUT&Tag). (b) X-ChIP: without crosslinking a short-lived TF is lost during digestion and washes. (c) N-ChIP: MNase gives mono-nucleosomes, so each read is one nucleosome. (d) X-ChIP with longer fixation and a double crosslinker: it does not bind DNA itself.

B9. Differences in crosslinking efficiency or epitope accessibility between the sites, residence time (a longer-bound site is captured more often), local shearing (open chromatin), copy number (site A amplified), mappability (part of site B in a repeat), and, at very strong sites, duplicates removed. Meaningful: the same site compared between samples processed the same way (differential binding).

C4. The two inputs are closest to the diagonal; the H3K36me3 input is the flattest. CTCF bends sharply at the very end: its top 1% of bins hold 30% of the reads (input 8%). That gradual bend is the fingerprint of a broad mark.

C5. H3K36me3 is enriched over the bodies of IFNAR1, IFNGR2, TMEM50B, GART, SON and DONSON, from a little after the TSS to the 3’ end, and not between genes. Solutions — R

R1. (a) It counts the 5’ ends of + and − strand reads at every base from 13 Mb to the end of chr21 and computes, for each shift k, the Pearson correlation between + strand counts at x and − strand counts at x + k, using only the positions with a + strand read for the sum of products (the others contribute zero). The first 13 Mb are the unassembled short arm and the centromere, with no reads. Reading the BAMs and computing all four curves takes about a minute. (b) CTCF: maximum 0.152 at 93 bp, the fragment length. CTCF input: 0.0021 at 95 bp, 70 times lower: a trace of the strongest sites (open CTCF sites are over-represented in the input), but essentially flat. H3K36me3: 0.0022 at 294 bp and its input 0.0007 at 331 bp: both noise; there is no fragment-length peak. H3K36me3 is above its input at every shift because both strands fall in the same domains. (c) At the read length (row 2, k = 36) CTCF has 0.092, on the rising flank towards the fragment peak (0.15 at 100); with a fragment length close to the read length, the phantom peak is hidden in the shoulder.

R2. (a) 72 active, 107 silent, 41 in between (220 in all). (b) CTCF input: 8.4× the expected count within 500 bp of active TSSs, 0.96× at silent TSSs. H3K36me3 input: 1.06× and 0.83×. The first input is strongly biased towards open promoters, the second hardly. TSSs and genes have a strand, and so do reads; without ignore.strand = TRUE, only reads on the same strand as the gene are counted, half of them.

R3. (a) Reads (5’ ends) per kb of gene body per million reads in the library. A uniform library gives 24.9 on chr21. (b) 69 active and 49 silent genes longer than 5 kb. The input is itself above average in active genes, so comparing the ChIP with the chromosome average would overstate the enrichment. (c) First 2 kb 30.5, last 2 kb 88.6: H3K36me3 is deposited by SETD2, which travels with the elongating RNA polymerase II, so the mark accumulates towards the 3’ end.

Where this leads

  • Peak calling turns these BAM files into peaks: MACS3’s local λ from the input, narrow vs broad calling for CTCF and H3K36me3, and replicates (IDR).
  • Annotation and differential binding asks where peaks are relative to genes and how binding differs between cell types.
  • CUT&RUN and CUT&Tag replace fragmentation and immunoprecipitation by an antibody-tethered enzyme.
