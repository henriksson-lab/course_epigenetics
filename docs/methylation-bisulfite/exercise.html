Exercise: Bisulfite and enzymatic sequencing

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Convert a DNA sequence by hand as bisulfite does, for both strands, and call methylation from aligned reads.
  • Decode Bismark’s methylation call string, compute a conversion rate and its effect on β, and decide which read positions to ignore from an M-bias plot.
  • Run a complete WGBS pipeline on the command line: trimming (fastp), a bisulfite index and alignment (Bismark), deduplication, methylation extraction, strand merging.
  • In R, read the M-bias and conversion statistics, build a per-CpG table, compare it with ENCODE’s calls, and see how SNPs masquerade as unmethylated CpGs.

Background

Conversion. Bisulfite (or the enzymes of EM-seq) turns unmethylated C into U, read as T after PCR; 5mC and 5hmC stay C. A read from the original top strand (OT) is the top strand with every unmethylated C replaced by T. A read from the original bottom strand (OB) is the same operation on the bottom strand, written 5’ to 3’.

Calls. At a reference C, a read with C is a methylated call, a read with T an unmethylated call. Bismark’s XM tag has one character per base: Z/z methylated/unmethylated CpG, X/x CHG, H/h CHH, U/u unknown context, . not a C.

Conversion rate. In most mammalian cells non-CpG cytosines are unmethylated, so the fraction of CHH calls that are “methylated” estimates the non-conversion rate \(f\). An observed level is then \(\beta_\text{obs} = \beta + (1-\beta) f\).

Precision. With \(N\) reads, the standard error of \(\hat\beta\) is \(\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)/N}\).

Data

From the course repository:

data/download.sh methylation-bisulfite      # about 45 MB
mkdir -p ~/epi/bisulfite && cd ~/epi/bisulfite
D=/path/to/course/data                      # where download.sh put the files
  • gm12878_wgbs_reads.fastq.gz: 360 142 read-1 sequences (125 bp, a few 151 bp; untrimmed) from ENCODE’s GM12878 WGBS replicate 1 (ENCSR890UQO, FASTQ ENCFF798RSS), a directional library.
  • hg38_chr21.fa.gz: the reference, chr21 of hg38.
  • gm12878_wgbs_rep1_chr21.bed.gz (+ .tbi): ENCODE’s per-CpG calls for the same replicate (see DNA methylation biology: column 10 coverage, 11 percent methylated, 13 called genotype).
  • gm12878_wgbs_pe_mbias.txt: an M-bias table (read 1 and read 2) computed by Bismark on 3 million random read pairs of the same replicate.

Shortcuts, said plainly. The reads are the ones ENCODE aligned to chr21:33–35 Mb, recovered from ENCODE’s alignment file and turned back into FASTQ (data/prepare-meth.sh). We align them to chr21 only. That keeps the run to a few minutes, but it inflates the alignment rate: reads from elsewhere in the genome are simply not in the file. A real analysis aligns all reads to the whole genome (and takes a day on a cluster node). Only read 1 is used, so the strong read-2 M-bias is shown with the separate table.

You need fastp and Bismark (which brings bowtie2 and samtools); see Install the software you need, or mamba install -c bioconda bismark fastp. The whole command-line part takes about 4–5 minutes on 8 threads (2–3 times longer on a laptop). On the cluster, run it in an interactive job or wrap the commands in an sbatch script with --cpus-per-task=8. The R part takes under a minute.

By hand

Do these on paper.

B1. Convert. The top strand of a piece of genome is 5’-ACGTCCGATCAG-3’. The first CpG (positions 2–3) is methylated on both strands; the second CpG (positions 6–7) is unmethylated; all other Cs are unmethylated.

  1. Write the bottom strand 5’ to 3’.

  2. Write the read you would get from the original top strand (OT) and from the original bottom strand (OB).

  3. After in-silico conversion of all C to T, which converted genome (C→T or G→A) does each read match?

B2. Call methylation. Six reads aligned to a reference; only the bases at three reference Cs are shown:

read C at 101 (CpG) C at 105 (CHH) C at 112 (CpG)
r1 C T C
r2 C T T
r3 T T C
r4 C T C
r5 C C T
r6 C T C
  1. Compute β at positions 101 and 112. (b) What is the most likely explanation for r5’s C at 105? (c) Write the XM characters for r2 at the three positions.

B3. Decode an XM tag. A read has XM:Z:..Z...hx..z....x..x.... How many methylated and unmethylated CpG calls, CHG calls and CHH calls does it contain? What fraction of its Cs are in CpG context?

B4. Conversion. (a) A library has 30 824 methylated and 4 613 214 unmethylated CHH calls. What is the non-conversion rate and the conversion rate? (b) What β is observed at a truly unmethylated CpG and at a CpG with true β = 0.5? (c) A second library has a lambda spike-in: 30 of 2000 lambda C calls are methylated. What conversion rate? Would you use it together with the first library in a comparison of unmethylated CpG islands? (d) Why can you not use CHH calls to estimate conversion in neurons or in Arabidopsis?

B5. Read the M-bias. CpG methylation by read position, from a paired-end library (the lecture plot):

position 1 2 3 4 5 6 median of 30–100
read 1 52.3 48.2 47.7 50.9 51.6 48.1 47.5
read 2 66.0 40.2 43.0 43.2 47.1 48.6 48.0
  1. Which positions would you ignore, in which read? (b) Why is read 2 affected more than read 1? (c) Why is this a bias and not biology?

B6. Coverage. (a) A CpG has 3 of 4 reads methylated, another 30 of 40. Both have β = 0.75. Give the approximate 95% interval of each. (b) How many reads do you need for a standard error of 0.05 at β = 0.5? (c) ENCODE asks for 30x per replicate. What is the standard error at β = 0.5 with 30 reads?

B7. Duplicates. (a) Our run removed 19% of aligned reads as duplicates. What would go wrong if you kept them? (b) Should you deduplicate an RRBS library? Why?

B8. A suspicious difference. In a comparison of two people, one CpG has β = 0.45 in person A (40 reads) and 0.90 in person B (40 reads); its neighbours are at 0.9 in both. (a) Give a technical explanation that involves the DNA sequence. (b) How would you check it in the BAM file of person A? (c) What would you do with such sites in a differential analysis?

B9. Choose an assay. Which method (WGBS, EM-seq, RRBS, EPIC array, nanopore, oxBS) would you choose for each, and why? (a) 1000 blood samples for an epigenome-wide association study. (b) 2 ng of cell-free DNA per patient, genome-wide. (c) Allele-specific methylation of an imprinted region in one person. (d) 5hmC vs 5mC in mouse cerebellum, genome-wide. (e) Promoter methylation in 300 tumours, cheaply, at single-CpG resolution.

In R

You need data.table and ggplot2.

suppressPackageStartupMessages({ library(data.table); library(ggplot2) })
D <- "/path/to/course/data"

R1 — M-bias

read_mbias <- function(file) {
  l <- readLines(file); out <- list(); section <- NA
  for (x in l) {
    if (grepl("context", x)) { section <- x; next }
    if (grepl("^[0-9]+\t", x)) {
      v <- as.numeric(strsplit(x, "\t")[[1]][1:3])
      out[[length(out) + 1]] <- data.table(section, pos = v[1], meth = v[2], unmeth = v[3])
    }
  }
  x <- rbindlist(out)
  x[, context := sub(" context.*", "", section)][, read := ifelse(grepl("R2", section), "R2", "R1")]
  x[, pct := 100 * meth / (meth + unmeth)][]
}
pe <- read_mbias(file.path(D, "gm12878_wgbs_pe_mbias.txt"))
se <- read_mbias("trimmed_bismark_bt2.deduplicated.M-bias.txt")
pe[context == "CpG" & pos <= 6, .(read, pos, pct = round(pct, 1))]
pe[context == "CpG" & pos %in% 30:100, .(plateau = median(pct)), by = read]
ggplot(pe[context == "CpG" & meth + unmeth > 2000], aes(pos, pct, colour = read)) + geom_line() +
  labs(x = "position in read", y = "% CpG methylated")
ggplot(se[context == "CpG"], aes(pos, pct)) + geom_line() + labs(x = "position in read 1", y = "% CpG methylated")
se[context == "CHH" & pos <= 3, .(pos, pct = round(pct, 2))]
  1. Which positions deviate from the plateau in each read of the paired-end library? (b) Is there any M-bias in our single-end read 1? Look at both CpG and CHH. (c) Write the Bismark option you would add to the extractor for the paired-end library.

R2 — Conversion

sp <- readLines("trimmed_bismark_bt2.deduplicated_splitting_report.txt")
get <- function(pat) as.numeric(sub(".*:\\s*", "", grep(pat, sp, value = TRUE)))
chh_m <- get("Total methylated C's in CHH"); chh_u <- get("Total C to T conversions in CHH")
f <- chh_m / (chh_m + chh_u)

beta_obs <- function(beta, f) beta + (1 - beta) * f
beta_obs(c(0, 0.5, 1), f)
beta_obs(c(0, 0.5, 1), 0.05)

What is the conversion rate? How much does it change β at 0 and at 0.5? What if it were only 95%?

R3 — The per-CpG table and its precision

ours <- fread("gm_cpg.CpG_report.merged_CpG_evidence.cov",
              col.names = c("chr", "start", "end", "pct", "M", "U"))
ours[, N := M + U][, beta := M / N]
nrow(ours); nrow(ours[start >= 33e6 & start < 35e6])
summary(ours$N); mean(ours$N >= 10)
ours[N >= 10, .(mean_beta = mean(beta), low = mean(beta < 0.2), high = mean(beta > 0.8))]
ggplot(ours[start >= 33e6 & start < 35e6], aes(pmin(N, 50))) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 1)

R5 — SNPs that look like unmethylated CpGs

x <- fread(cmd = paste("tabix", file.path(D, "gm12878_wgbs_rep1_chr21.bed.gz"), "chr21:33000001-35000000"),
           header = FALSE, select = c(2, 6, 13), col.names = c("s0", "strand", "call"))

o <- merge(ours[N >= 8], snp[, .(start, type)], by = "start", all.x = TRUE)

  1. What does ENCODE’s genotype code YG mean (IUPAC: Y = C or T)? And TG? (b) Compare the methylation that Bismark reports at each type of site. Explain the numbers. (c) Why do “other” SNPs (e.g. G/T at the G) lower β less?

Hints

Hint 1 — conversion by hand (B1, B2, B3)

Write the bottom strand as the reverse complement of the top. Then, on each strand separately, replace every C that is not methylated by T. A CpG on the top strand at positions \(i, i+1\) has its bottom-strand C opposite position \(i+1\). The Bismark user guide explains the XM characters and the four strands (alignment modes).

Hint 2 — conversion rate and M-bias (B4, B5, R1, R2)

Non-conversion = methylated CHH / all CHH calls. A spike-in of unmethylated lambda DNA gives the same estimate where CHH methylation is real. The M-bias plot was introduced with BSmooth (Hansen et al. 2012); Bismark’s extractor has --ignore, --ignore_r2, --ignore_3prime and --ignore_3prime_r2 (manual).

Hint 3 — precision (B6, R3)

95% interval ≈ \(\hat\beta \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat\beta(1-\hat\beta)/N}\); for small \(N\) use the exact interval (binom.test). Solve \(\sqrt{0.25/N} = 0.05\) for \(N\).

Hint 4 — tools (C2–C8)

fastp trims adapters and low-quality reads; Bismark documents bismark_genome_preparation, bismark, deduplicate_bismark, bismark_methylation_extractor and coverage2cytosine. tabix extracts a region from an indexed file. XR and XG are explained in the Bismark output description.

Hint 5 — SNPs and assay choice (B8, B9, R5)

IUPAC codes: Y = C/T, R = A/G, K = G/T, M = A/C, S = C/G. Bisulfite cannot create an A or a G, or turn a G into anything else. The lecture’s “Choosing an assay” table and Methylation arrays help with B9.

Solutions

Solutions — by hand

B1. (a) Bottom strand 5’→3’: CTGATCGGACGT. (b) OT: ACGTTTGATTAG (the C at position 2 stays C, the Cs at 5, 6 and 10 become T). OB: TTGATTGGACGT (bottom-strand Cs at its positions 1 and 6 become T; the C at position 10, which pairs with the G of the methylated first CpG, stays C). (c) OT, after C→T, matches the C→T genome (top strand); OB, after C→T, matches the G→A genome (the top strand with G→A is the reverse complement of the C→T-converted bottom strand).

B2. (a) 101: 5 C of 6 = 0.83; 112: 4 of 6 = 0.67. (b) Incomplete conversion of that molecule (or a sequencing error); in mammals CHH methylation is near 0. A read with several unconverted non-CpG Cs is usually removed (Bismark’s filter_non_conversion). (c) r2: Z, h, z.

B3. One methylated CpG (Z), one unmethylated CpG (z), three unmethylated CHG (x), one unmethylated CHH (h); six C calls, of which 2/6 = 33% in CpG context.

B4. (a) \(f = 30\,824/4\,644\,038 = 0.66\%\); conversion 99.34%. (b) \(0 + 1 \times 0.0066 = 0.0066\); \(0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.0066 = 0.503\). (c) \(30/2000 = 1.5\%\), conversion 98.5%: acceptable by ENCODE’s ≥ 98%, but unmethylated islands would read about 1.5% vs 0.7% in the two libraries, a small systematic difference. Fine for most purposes; correct for it or treat library as a covariate if you compare low-methylation regions between batches. (d) Neurons have real non-CpG (mostly CpA) methylation, and plants methylate CHG and CHH: methylated CHH calls are then biology, not failed conversion. Use an unmethylated spike-in.

B5. (a) Read 2: ignore positions 1–4 (1 is too high, 2–4 too low; from 5 on it is near the 48% plateau): --ignore_r2 4. Read 1: position 1 is slightly high (52.3) and 4–5 slightly noisy; ignoring the first 1–2 bases is a cautious choice (--ignore 2). (b) End repair fills in the fragment end that read 2 starts from with unmethylated C; read 1 starts at the other end. (c) The methylation of a CpG cannot depend on where in a read it happens to be observed; averaged over millions of reads, every position should show the same level.

B6. (a) 3/4: \(0.75 \pm 1.96\sqrt{0.1875/4} = 0.75 \pm 0.42\) (the normal approximation fails here; the exact interval is 0.19–0.99). 30/40: \(0.75 \pm 1.96\sqrt{0.1875/40} = 0.75 \pm 0.13\) (0.62–0.88). (b) \(\sqrt{0.25/N} = 0.05 \Rightarrow N = 100\). (c) \(\sqrt{0.25/30} = 0.091\).

B7. (a) Copies of one molecule count as independent observations: coverage and confidence are inflated, and one molecule’s methylation is over-weighted (pseudo-replication, as in differential accessibility). (b) No: RRBS fragments all start at MspI sites, so identical starts are expected and removing them would remove most real data (UMIs can identify true duplicates).

B8. (a) Person A is heterozygous for a C→T SNP at the CpG: the T allele reads as “unmethylated”, halving β. (b) Look at reads from the other strand (OB if the SNP is at the top-strand C): at the opposite base they show G (reference) vs A (SNP), unchanged by bisulfite. A SNP caller for bisulfite data (Bis-SNP, BISCUIT, gemBS) does this systematically; or check dbSNP. (c) Remove CpGs that overlap known common SNPs or SNPs called in the samples before testing, or analyse them as meQTLs with genotype in the model.

B9. (a) EPIC array: cheapest per sample, standardised, 900 000 CpGs chosen for relevance; good statistical power from many samples. (b) EM-seq: works on picograms to nanograms with little damage (bisulfite would destroy much of 2 ng). (c) Nanopore or PacBio long reads: one read spans the region and heterozygous SNPs, so methylation is assigned to each parental haplotype. (d) oxBS together with BS (or TAB-seq), or nanopore: bisulfite or EM-seq alone report 5mC + 5hmC. (e) RRBS (or a targeted panel): covers most CpG-island promoters at single-CpG resolution cheaply; arrays are an alternative if the promoters of interest are on the array.

R5. (a) YG: at the C of this CpG the cell line is heterozygous C/T (the CpG exists on one allele only). TG: homozygous T: there is no CpG on either allele. (CR and CA are the same SNPs seen from the other strand.) In the region ENCODE called 328 heterozygous, 84 homozygous and 343 other SNP CpGs. (c) A G/T or C/A change produces a base that bisulfite conversion cannot produce (A at a C, or T at the G read on the other strand): Bismark treats it as a mismatch and does not count it as an unmethylated call, so β drops less.

Where this leads

  • Differential methylation takes per-CpG counts like those of C8 from several samples (ENCODE’s GM12878 and K562 replicates) and tests where they differ.
  • Methylation arrays measure selected CpGs with bisulfite conversion and hybridisation instead of sequencing.
  • Methylation and chromatin from long reads reads methylation without conversion, on single molecules and haplotypes.
  • At scale, nf-core/methylseq runs the same steps (trim, Bismark or bwa-meth, deduplicate, extract, MultiQC) on a cluster.
