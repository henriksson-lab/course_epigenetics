Exercise: Gene regulation and TF motifs

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Build a position weight matrix (PWM) from aligned binding sites with pseudocounts, and score sequences with it on both strands, by hand.
  • Compute the information content of a motif column and read a sequence logo.
  • Estimate how many chance matches a motif has in a region or a genome, and use that to judge claims about TF binding.
  • Fetch a matrix from JASPAR in R, scan peak sequences and backgrounds with motifmatchr, and measure enrichment, centrality and the fraction of bound matches.

Background

PWM. From \(N\) aligned sites, count each base \(b\) at each position \(j\) (\(n_{b,j}\)), add a pseudocount, and divide: \(p_{b,j} = (n_{b,j} + 1) / (N + 4)\) with a pseudocount of 1 per base. The log-odds weight against a background frequency \(q_b\) is \(w_{b,j} = \log_2 (p_{b,j} / q_b)\). The score of a sequence window is the sum of the weights of its bases. Scan both strands: the minus strand is the reverse complement.

Information content of column \(j\) against a uniform background: \(IC_j = 2 + \sum_b p_{b,j} \log_2 p_{b,j}\) bits, between 0 (no preference) and 2 (one base only). A logo stacks the letters of each column to height \(IC_j\).

Chance matches. An exact \(k\)-mer occurs with probability \(4^{-k}\) per position and strand in random sequence. For a PWM scanned at a \(p\)-value threshold \(p\), a sequence of length \(L\) has about \(2Lp\) matches by chance. Roughly, a motif with \(I\) bits of information is found about once every \(2^I\) bp per strand.

Bound or not. A TF binds only a small fraction of its motif matches: accessibility, nucleosomes, DNA methylation, partner TFs and TF concentration decide which.

Data

GM12878 CTCF peaks (ENCODE, IDR conservative, narrowPeak), GM12878 ATAC-seq peaks, the hg38 genome as a bgzipped, indexed FASTA (hg38.fa.bgz with .fai and .gzi, so that any region can be read without unpacking the whole file), the hg38 chromosome sizes and the ENCODE blacklist. From the course repository:

data/download.sh gene-regulation
mkdir -p ~/epi/generegulation && cd ~/epi/generegulation
D=/path/to/course/data                  # where download.sh put the files

You need samtools (1.10 or newer), bedtools and, in R, the Bioconductor packages JASPAR2024, TFBSTools, motifmatchr, universalmotif, Rsamtools, Biostrings, GenomicRanges and rtracklayer (see Install the software you need). The R part runs in about two minutes on a laptop.

Simplification: we use one matrix per TF and a single \(p\)-value threshold (\(10^{-4}\), uniform background). Motifs, motif enrichment and TF footprinting does this properly, with motif databases, enrichment statistics and footprints.

By hand

Do these on paper. A calculator for \(\log_2\) helps: \(\log_2 x = \ln x / \ln 2\); \(\log_2 3 = 1.585\), \(\log_2 (2/3) = -0.585\), \(\log_2 (1/3) = -1.585\).

B1. Build a PWM. Eight binding sites of a GATA-like factor, aligned:

AGATAA   TGATAA   AGATAG   AGATAA
TGATAG   AGATTA   AGATAA   CGATAA

  1. Write the 4 × 6 count matrix (rows A, C, G, T).

  2. Convert it to frequencies with a pseudocount of 1 per base. Why is a pseudocount needed here?

  3. Compute the log-odds weights against a uniform background (\(q_b = 0.25\)). Write down the weight matrix; you will need it in B2.

B2. Score sequences. Using your weights from B1:

  1. Score AGATAA, TGATAG, AGCTAA and GGGGGG. What are the highest and the lowest possible scores?

  2. A sequence on the plus strand reads TTATCT. What does it score as written? What does it score on the minus strand? Where is the motif, and which way does it point?

  3. With a threshold of 6 bits, which of the five sequences are matches?

B3. Information content.

  1. Compute \(IC\) for column 1 and for column 2 of your PWM (with the pseudocounts).

  2. What would \(IC\) of column 2 be without pseudocounts? Why does the pseudocount matter so much more with 8 sites than with 913 (the number of sites behind the JASPAR CTCF matrix)?

  3. A column is 50% A and 50% G, never C or T. What is its \(IC\)? How tall is each letter in the logo?

B4. How many matches by chance? Take the human genome as \(3.1 \times 10^9\) bp of random sequence with equal base frequencies.

  1. How often do you expect the exact 6-mer AGATAA per Mb (both strands)? In the whole genome?

  2. Repeat for the E-box CACGTG. Why is the answer half of (a)? (Write out its reverse complement.)

  3. A PWM is scanned with a threshold of \(p < 10^{-4}\) per position and strand. How many matches do you expect on chr21 (40 Mb of sequence) and in the genome? GM12878 has 44 217 CTCF peaks: what is the largest possible fraction of genome-wide CTCF matches that can be bound?

  4. The CTCF matrix has 16.1 bits of information. Using the rule of thumb, about how often does a CTCF site of that quality occur by chance?

B5. Strand and orientation. A scan of chr1 with the CTCF matrix reports four matches (1-based start, strand):

site 1   1 000 001   +
site 2   1 180 001   −
site 3   1 400 001   −
site 4   1 650 001   +

  1. The JASPAR consensus is GCCACCAGGGGGCGC. Write the plus-strand sequence of the genome at site 2 if it matched the consensus perfectly.

  2. Draw the four sites as arrows. Which pairs are convergent? Loop extrusion by cohesin predicts loops between which sites?

  3. Site 2 is deleted. Which loop do you now expect?

B6. Read a logo. Look at the CTCF logo on the Information content and sequence logos slide (JASPAR MA0139.2, 913 sites).

  1. Which three positions carry the most information? About how many bits each?

  2. Position 9 has G (566 sites) and A (333 sites). What does a mixed column mean for binding? Is a site with A at position 9 a “mutant” site?

  3. Why are the end columns low?

  4. A logo built from only 20 sites without pseudocounts looks “cleaner”, with taller columns. Is that motif more specific?

B7. Judge the claims.

  1. “The CTCF motif is the main determinant of CTCF binding, so CTCF binds essentially every match to its motif.” Use your answer to B4c and the chr21 numbers from the lecture (8583 matches, 369 inside a CTCF peak) to judge the claim.

  2. A paper reports: “TF X has 600 000 motif matches but only 12 000 ChIP-seq peaks. Therefore 98% of its sites are silenced by DNA methylation.” What else could explain the gap? What experiment would test the methylation claim?

  3. A student finds the PU.1 motif in 22% of GM12878 ATAC peaks and concludes that PU.1 binds 22% of the open chromatin in B cells. What is missing from this reasoning? (In the lecture: 12% of GC-matched background windows also have a match.)

B8. Read the figure. You ChIP two TFs, Y and Z, and plot the position of the best match to each one’s motif relative to the summit, as on the centrality slide. Y gives a sharp peak at 0; Z gives a flat histogram, although Z’s motif is found in 40% of its peaks (15% in background). Give two explanations for Z.

for f in ctcf_win shuf_win; do
  echo $f \
    $(grep -c -E 'CCCTC|GAGGG' $f.txt) \
    $(grep -c -E 'CCA[CG][CT]AG[AG][GT]GG|CC[AC][CT]CT[AG][CG]TGG' $f.txt) \

R6 — Where in the peak is the motif?

Listing every match position is slow for 44 000 sequences, so take a random 5000 peaks and a 500-bp window:

set.seed(1)

  2. Why + 7 and − 250.5? (The motif is 15 bp; the window positions run from 1 to 500.)

  3. Are the matches mostly on one strand? What would you expect, and why does strand still matter for CTCF (B5)?

Hints

Hint 1 — PWMs and logos (B1–B3, B6, R2)

Work column by column. With 8 sites and a pseudocount of 1 per base, every frequency is \((n + 1)/12\), so only four values occur: 1/12, 2/12, 3/12 … 9/12. For the information content, \(-p \log_2 p\) is 0.5 for \(p = 0.5\), 0.5 for \(p = 0.25\), 0.311 for \(p = 0.75\), 0.431 for \(p = 1/6\), 0.299 for \(p = 1/12\). The original papers: Stormo 2000 (PWMs), Schneider & Stephens 1990 (logos).

Hint 2 — chance matches and strands (B4, B5)

Expected count = (number of positions) × (strands) × (probability per position and strand). A palindromic word (equal to its own reverse complement) is the same match on both strands, so it should be counted once. A match on the − strand reads the motif on the reverse complement: write the consensus, then reverse it and complement each base. Convergent CTCF sites: a + site on the left and a − site on the right, pointing towards each other (see the CTCF loop-extrusion slide and Rao et al. 2014).

Hint 3 — command line (C1, C2)

Column 10 of a narrowPeak file is the summit offset from the start (format). samtools faidx reads regions written as chr:start-end (1-based, inclusive) from a bgzipped, indexed FASTA; -r takes them from a file and -n sets the line length. bedtools shuffle with -chrom -excl is the restricted shuffle of Genomic intervals. In a regular expression [CG] means C or G.

Hint 4 — JASPAR and motifmatchr (R1–R7)

JASPAR2024 is a database package; TFBSTools::getMatrixByID reads one matrix from it, and the matrix page is on the JASPAR website. motifmatchr::matchMotifs scans sequences: out = "matches" (default) gives TRUE/FALSE per sequence, out = "positions" the coordinates, strand and score of every match; bg = "even" uses a uniform background, p.cutoff the threshold. Rsamtools::FaFile and getSeq read sequences of a GRanges from an indexed FASTA. universalmotif has shuffle_sequences() (with k = 2 it keeps dinucleotide counts) and view_motifs(). Biostrings has letterFrequency(), consensusMatrix() and reverseComplement().

Hint 5 — judging claims (B7, B8)

Compare the number of matches with the number of peaks, and ask what else differs between bound and unbound matches: accessibility, nucleosomes, methylation, partners, TF level. For a causal claim about methylation, think of an experiment that changes methylation and measures binding (e.g. a DNMT inhibitor or DNMT knockout, or methylation-sensitive binding in vitro). For enrichment, always compare with a background with the same composition.

Solutions

Solutions — by hand

B1. (a) Counts (columns 1–6):

A   5  0  8  0  7  6
C   1  0  0  0  0  0
G   0  8  0  0  0  2
T   2  0  0  8  1  0

  1. \(p = (n + 1)/12\): column 1 is A 0.50, C 0.17, G 0.08, T 0.25; columns 2–4 have 0.75 for the consensus base and 0.08 for the others; column 5 is A 0.67, T 0.17, C and G 0.08; column 6 is A 0.58, G 0.25, C and T 0.08. Without a pseudocount every unseen base has frequency 0 and weight \(-\infty\), so a single unseen base would veto a site. Eight sites are far too few to call a base impossible.

  2. Weights \(\log_2(p / 0.25)\):

A   1.00  -1.58   1.58  -1.58   1.42   1.22
C  -0.58  -1.58  -1.58  -1.58  -1.58  -1.58
G  -1.58   1.58  -1.58  -1.58  -1.58   0.00
T   0.00  -1.58  -1.58   1.58  -0.58  -1.58

B2. (a) AGATAA = 1.00 + 1.58 + 1.58 + 1.58 + 1.42 + 1.22 = 8.39 (the maximum: the best base in every column). TGATAG = 0 + 1.58 + 1.58 + 1.58 + 1.42 + 0 = 6.17. AGCTAA = 1.00 + 1.58 − 1.58 + 1.58 + 1.42 + 1.22 = 5.22. GGGGGG = −1.58 + 1.58 − 1.58 − 1.58 − 1.58 + 0 = −4.75. Minimum: −1.58 in every column = −9.51. (b) As written, TTATCT scores 0 − 1.58 + 1.58 − 1.58 − 0.58 − 1.58 = −1.58. Its reverse complement is AGATAA, which scores 8.39: the motif is on the minus strand, reading right to left on the genome. (c) AGATAA, TGATAG, and TTATCT on the minus strand pass 6 bits. AGCTAA (5.22) is a near miss: a single mismatch at a 1.58-bit position.

B3. (a) Column 1: \(p = (0.50, 0.17, 0.08, 0.25)\); \(\sum -p\log_2 p = 0.5 + 0.431 + 0.299 + 0.5 = 1.73\), so $IC = 2 - 1.73 = $ 0.27 bits. Column 2: \(p = (0.75, 0.08, 0.08, 0.08)\); \(0.311 + 3 \times 0.299 = 1.21\), $IC = $ 0.79 bits. (b) Without pseudocounts column 2 is 100% G: 2 bits. With 8 sites the pseudocount (4 extra “observations”) is a third of the data; with 913 sites it is 0.4%. CTCF’s column 3 (C in 903 of 913 sites) has 1.9 bits with or without it. (c) \(\sum -p\log_2 p = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1\), $IC = $ 1 bit. The stack is 1 bit tall; A and G are each 0.5 bit.

B4. (a) \(4^{-6} = 1/4096\) per position and strand. Per Mb: \(2 \times 10^6 / 4096 \approx\) 490. Genome: \(2 \times 3.1 \times 10^9 / 4096 \approx\) 1.5 million. (b) CACGTG is its own reverse complement (a palindrome): each occurrence is found on both strands but is one site, so about 245 per Mb and 760 000 per genome. (c) chr21: $2 ^6 ^{-4} = $ 8000 (8018 for the exact 40.1 Mb; 8583 found in R7). Genome: \(2 \times 3.1 \times 10^9 \times 10^{-4} \approx\) 620 000. Even if every one of the 44 217 peaks held a different match, at most \(44\,217 / 620\,000 \approx\) 7% of matches could be bound. (d) \(2^{16.1} \approx 70\,000\) bp per strand, so about one per 35 kb counting both strands: roughly 30 per Mb, 90 000 in the genome. Still twice the number of CTCF peaks.

B5. (a) A − strand match means the consensus reads on the reverse complement. The plus strand at site 2 reads the reverse complement of GCCACCAGGGGGCGC, which is GCGCCCCCTGGTGGC. (b) Sites: 1 →, 2 ←, 3 ←, 4 →. Convergent pairs (→ … ←): 1–2 and 1–3. Site 4 points away from all the others to its right (none). Cohesin extruding from between 1 and 2 stalls at 1 and 2: the loop is 1–2. 1–3 can form if cohesin passes site 2 (CTCF is not bound at every site in every cell). (c) Without site 2, cohesin stalls at 1 and 3: a larger loop 1–3. (Deleting or inverting CTCF sites re-wires loops in exactly this way.)

B6. (a) Positions 3 (C), 8 (G) and 11 (G), each about 1.9 bits (R2: 1.90, 1.91, 1.82). Position 7 (A, 1.55 bits) is next. (b) Both G and A are tolerated at position 9; G is preferred, but a site with A is a normal site with a slightly lower score, not a mutant. A logo shows a distribution of preferences, not one “correct” sequence. (c) The edges of the motif contact the protein less, and the alignment of sites is less precise at the edges. (d) No. With 20 sites and no pseudocount, unseen bases get frequency 0 and columns look like 2 bits by sampling alone. The apparent specificity is an artefact of the small sample.

B7. (a) The claim fails. On chr21 only 369 of 8583 matches (4.3%) are in a CTCF peak; genome-wide at most 7% could be (B4c). The motif is close to necessary (79% of peaks have a match, R7) but far from sufficient: accessibility, nucleosomes, methylation and the rest of the chromatin context decide. (b) Most matches are expected by chance (B4); most lie in closed chromatin or on nucleosomes; many are weak; X may need a partner; ChIP detects only sites occupied in many cells at once; and X’s concentration may limit occupancy. To test methylation: compare binding before and after removing DNA methylation (DNMT1/3A/3B knockout or a DNMT inhibitor) and check whether new peaks appear at matches that were methylated; or test methylated vs unmethylated DNA in vitro. (c) A background. 12% of GC-matched random windows also have a PU.1 match, so most of the 22% would be there by chance; the enrichment is 1.8×. A match is also not binding: PU.1 ChIP-seq is needed to say where PU.1 is bound.

B8. Z may bind DNA indirectly, through a partner whose motif is at the summit, while Z’s motif is enriched in the peaks because Z and its partner co-occur in the same regulatory regions. Or Z’s matrix is wrong or describes a related TF whose motif is common in such regions (e.g. a GC-rich motif in GC-rich peaks). An antibody problem (the ChIP pulls down another protein) gives the same picture.

(b) Flanks keep the regional context (same chromosome, nearby DNA) but not the composition: their GC is 0.445, lower than the peaks’ 0.514. Where this leads

  • The ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq blocks produce the peaks that we scanned here, from raw reads.
  • Motifs, motif enrichment and TF footprinting turns R4 into a proper test over hundreds of matrices (HOMER, MEME-SEA, chromVAR) and looks for TF footprints in ATAC data.
  • The 3D genome units show the CTCF loops of B5 in Hi-C maps.
  • The machine-learning units train a network to tell CTCF peaks from GC-matched background: its first layer learns PWM-like filters.
