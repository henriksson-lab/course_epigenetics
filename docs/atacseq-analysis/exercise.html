Exercise: ATAC-seq processing and peak calling

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Turn a read pair into its two Tn5 insertion sites by hand (+4/−5) and on the command line.
  • Choose a local λ and compute a Poisson p-value for a candidate peak, and explain what a q-value adds.
  • Process paired-end ATAC-seq reads from FASTQ to a filtered BAM with fastp, Bowtie2 and samtools, and say what each filter removes.
  • Call peaks with MACS3 on fragments and on insertion sites, and compare the two.
  • Compute FRiP, compare your peaks with ENCODE’s, and judge replicate agreement by rank consistency.
  • Build a fixed-width consensus peak set (500 bp around summits, iterative overlap removal) in R and annotate it relative to TSSs.

Background

Insertion sites. Tn5 cuts the two DNA strands 9 bp apart, and both resulting fragments carry a copy of those 9 bp. The insertion is taken as the centre of the 9 bp: in BED coordinates the start + 4 of a read on the + strand and the end − 5 of a read on the − strand. A properly paired fragment [s, e) therefore has insertion sites at bases s + 4 and e − 5.

Peak calling. MACS3 piles up reads (or windows around insertions) and, at each position, compares the pileup \(k\) with an expected count \(\lambda\) under background, \(p = P(X \ge k)\) for \(X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\). The expectation is local: \(\lambda_\text{local} = \max(\lambda_\text{BG}, \lambda_\text{1k}, \lambda_\text{5k}, \lambda_\text{10k})\), where each \(\lambda\) is the count in a window around the position, scaled to the size of the counting window. Without a control (ATAC-seq), MACS3 uses \(\lambda_\text{BG}\) and the 10-kb window of the sample itself. P-values are converted to q-values (Benjamini–Hochberg).

Replicates and consensus. Overlap between two replicates’ peak lists depends on the thresholds; IDR instead asks how far down the ranked lists the two replicates agree. To count reads in many samples you need one set of regions: either merged peaks or fixed-width peaks (summit ± 250 bp) where overlapping peaks are resolved by keeping the most significant one.

Data

ENCODE ATAC-seq of the lymphoblastoid cell line GM12878 (experiment ENCSR095QNB, GRCh38), two biological replicates, paired-end 2 × 100 bp. To keep the exercise small we use only the read pairs that ENCODE aligned to chromosome 21 or the mitochondrial genome; the reads themselves are the original, untrimmed reads. You also need the chr21 + chrM reference with its Bowtie2 index, ENCODE’s IDR peaks for the same experiment, the blacklist, the GENCODE annotation and chromosome sizes.

You need fastp, bowtie2, samtools (1.16 or later), bedtools, macs3 and, for the optional bigWig, bedGraphToBigWig (see Install the software you need). By hand

Do these on paper.

B1. Tn5 shift. A read pair, as shown by samtools view (1-based POS):

read 1   flag 99    chr21   POS 10001   CIGAR 50M   (+ strand)
read 2   flag 147   chr21   POS 10231   CIGAR 50M   (− strand)

  1. Write both reads as BED intervals. How long is the fragment (TLEN)?

  2. Give the two insertion sites as one-base BED intervals. How far apart are they?

  3. After adapter trimming, a − strand read has POS 20001 and CIGAR 30M. Where is its insertion site?

  4. A script shifts every − strand read by subtracting 5 from its start. Where would the insertion from read 2 end up, and how far is that from the correct site? What would it be for an untrimmed 100-bp read?

  5. Why +4 but −5? What are the two offsets in 1-based coordinates?

B2. The genome-wide λ. After filtering, a sample has 2 000 000 insertion sites on chr21, whose mappable length is about 40 Mb. MACS3 replaces each insertion by a 150-bp window.

  1. What is \(\lambda_\text{BG}\), the expected number of windows covering a position under uniform background?

  2. What would \(\lambda_\text{BG}\) be if you forgot to set the genome size and MACS3 used the human genome (2.7 × 10⁹)?

  3. Why does this mistake change the peaks less than you might expect? (Think about \(\lambda_\text{local}\).)

B3. Local λ and a Poisson p-value. At a candidate peak summit the pileup is \(k = 18\). \(\lambda_\text{BG} = 4.0\). The 10-kb window around the summit contains 600 insertions. A ChIP-seq-style control gives \(\lambda_\text{1k} = 6.0\) and \(\lambda_\text{5k} = 7.5\). Upper-tail Poisson probabilities \(P(X \ge k \mid \lambda)\):

\(k\) \ \(\lambda\) 4 6 7.5 9
12 9.2e-04 2.0e-02 7.9e-02 0.20
15 2.0e-05 1.4e-03 1.0e-02 0.041
18 2.5e-07 5.7e-05 7.9e-04 0.0053
21 1.9e-09 1.5e-06 3.9e-05 0.00044

  1. Compute \(\lambda_\text{10k}\) (scale the 10-kb count to the 150-bp window).

  2. What is \(\lambda_\text{local}\) with the control? Without a control (ATAC-seq, MACS3)?

  3. What p-value does the peak get? What would it have got against \(\lambda_\text{BG}\) alone?

  4. Why does MACS take the maximum of the λ estimates, not the mean?

B4. p-values and q-values. MACS3 tests about 10⁶ independent windows on chr21. 2000 windows have \(p < 10^{-4}\).

  1. How many windows would you expect below \(p < 10^{-4}\) if there were no real peaks at all?

  2. Estimate the fraction of false discoveries among the 2000 (this is roughly the Benjamini–Hochberg q-value at that cutoff).

  3. Why is -p 0.01 a bad final threshold, but a good input for IDR?

B5. FRiP. Replicate A: 3 100 000 insertions after filtering, 1 050 000 inside peaks.

  1. Compute FRiP. Does it pass the ENCODE standard (> 0.3, > 0.2 acceptable)?

  2. You call peaks again with -p 0.01 instead of -q 0.01; FRiP rises to 0.41. Is the library better now? What does this tell you about comparing FRiP between studies?

B6. Replicate overlap. Replicate 1 has 4000 peaks, replicate 2 has 5000. 3200 of replicate 1’s peaks overlap a replicate-2 peak; 3350 of replicate 2’s peaks overlap a replicate-1 peak.

  1. Give the fraction reproduced in each direction. Why are 3200 and 3350 not the same number?

  2. You lower the threshold in replicate 1 to get 8000 peaks; now 3900 of them overlap replicate 2. What happened to the fraction, and why?

  3. Explain in two sentences what IDR does differently.

B7. A fixed-width consensus by hand. Summits (BED position of the summit base) and scores (−log10 p, already normalised between samples) from two samples on chr1:

sample summit score
S1 1000 40
S1 1350 12
S1 5000 25
S2 1200 30
S2 4700 18

  1. Write the 500-bp windows [summit − 250, summit + 250).

  2. Apply iterative overlap removal: which windows are kept?

  3. What would bedtools merge of the six windows return? Compare the number and the widths of the regions.

  4. Give two reasons why fixed-width peaks are easier to use for a count matrix than merged peaks.

B8. Judge the methods. A study merges the MACS3 peaks of 40 samples with bedtools merge and reports “120 000 accessible regions, median width 1.8 kb”. The peaks of each sample had a median width of 450 bp. What most likely happened, and what would you suggest?

B9. Read the figure. In the lecture’s rank-consistency plot (replicate 1 vs 2, Real data: do the replicates agree?), about how many of replicate 1’s strongest peaks can you take before fewer than 90% of them are found in replicate 2? On the command line

C1 — Trim and align

The same block for both replicates (5–12 min per replicate with 8 threads, depending on the machine):

for r in rep1 rep2; do
  fastp -w $T -l 20 \
    -i $D/gm12878_atac_${r}_R1.fastq.gz -I $D/gm12878_atac_${r}_R2.fastq.gz \
    -o ${r}_R1.trim.fq.gz -O ${r}_R2.trim.fq.gz \
    --adapter_sequence CTGTCTCTTATACACATCT --adapter_sequence_r2 CTGTCTCTTATACACATCT \
    -j ${r}.fastp.json -h ${r}.fastp.html 2> ${r}.fastp.log
  bowtie2 -p $T --very-sensitive -X 2000 -x $D/bowtie2/hg38_chr21_chrM \
    -1 ${r}_R1.trim.fq.gz -2 ${r}_R2.trim.fq.gz 2> ${r}.bowtie2.log \
    | samtools fixmate -m -u - - | samtools sort -@ 4 -o ${r}.raw.bam
  samtools index ${r}.raw.bam
done

  1. Open rep1.fastp.html (or read rep1.fastp.log). How many read pairs went in? What fraction of reads had adapter bases trimmed? fastp also reports an “insert size peak”: why is it so short?

  2. From rep1.bowtie2.log: what fraction of pairs aligned concordantly exactly once, and overall?

  3. Rerun the bowtie2 command for replicate 1 without -X 2000 (send the output to /dev/null, keep the log). How does the concordant fraction change? Which fragments are now discordant?

C4 — Call peaks in two modes

mkdir -p macs
for r in rep1 rep2; do
  macs3 callpeak -t ${r}.dedup.bam -f BAMPE -g 4.0e7 --keep-dup all -q 0.01 \
    -n ${r}_frag --outdir macs 2> ${r}_frag.macs.log
  macs3 callpeak -t ${r}.ins.bed -f BED --nomodel --shift -75 --extsize 150 \
    -g 4.0e7 --keep-dup all -q 0.01 -n ${r}_cuts --outdir macs 2> ${r}_cuts.macs.log

done

R2 — A fixed-width consensus with iterative overlap removal

fixed <- function(p, sample) {
  s <- GRanges(seqnames(p), IRanges(start(p) + p$peak, width = 1))   # summit base (1-based)
  w <- resize(s, 500, fix = "center")                                # 500 bp around it
  w$score <- p$pValue / sum(p$pValue) * 1e6                          # score per million
  w$sample <- sample
  w
}
keep_best <- function(gr) {
  gr <- gr[order(-gr$score)]
  ov <- as.list(findOverlaps(gr, gr))           # for each peak, the peaks it overlaps
  keep <- logical(length(gr)); gone <- logical(length(gr))
  for (i in seq_along(gr)) {
    if (gone[i]) next
    keep[i] <- TRUE
    gone[ov[[i]]] <- TRUE                       # itself and every overlapping peak
  }
  sort(gr[keep])
}
q1 <- np("macs/rep1_cuts_peaks.narrowPeak"); q2 <- np("macs/rep2_cuts_peaks.narrowPeak")
per_sample <- c(keep_best(fixed(q1, "rep1")), keep_best(fixed(q2, "rep2")))
cons <- keep_best(per_sample)
length(cons); table(width(cons))
# reproducible: overlapped by a fixed-width peak of both replicates
n_rep <- sapply(split(per_sample, per_sample$sample), function(x) overlapsAny(cons, x))
cons_rep <- cons[rowSums(n_rep) == 2]
length(cons_rep)
merged <- import("merged.bed")
c(merged = length(merged), median_width = median(width(merged)))
export(cons_rep, "consensus_500bp.bed")

  1. Why does fixed() use start(p) + p$peak (check with Genomic intervals: is that the 1-based summit position)?

  2. How many 500-bp peaks are kept per sample, in the consensus, and in the reproducible consensus? Compare with the merged set from C7 (number and widths).

  3. Why is the score normalised per million before peaks from different samples are compared?

  4. Check that no two peaks in cons overlap (last line of R3’s code, or run it now).

Hints

Hint 1 — the Tn5 shift (B1, C3)

SAM POS is 1-based: BED start = POS − 1. A read’s BED end = BED start + aligned length (sum of M, D, =, X in the CIGAR). On the + strand the 5’ end is the start, on the − strand the end. Insertion = start + 4 (+ strand) or end − 5 (− strand), written as a one-base BED interval [x, x + 1). See Buenrostro et al. 2013 and bedtools bamtobed -bedpe; the deepTools tool alignmentSieve --ATACshift does the same on a BAM.

Hint 2 — λ and Poisson (B2, B3, B4)

\(\lambda_\text{BG} = N \times w / G\) for \(N\) reads, window \(w\) and genome size \(G\). A count in a larger window \(W\) is scaled to \(w\): \(\lambda_W = n_W \times w / W\). \(\lambda_\text{local}\) is the maximum. Expected false positives at a cutoff = number of tests × cutoff. See the MACS3 callpeak documentation (--slocal, --llocal, -g) and Zhang et al. 2008.

Hint 3 — filters (C2)

samtools view -q 30 keeps MAPQ ≥ 30, -f 2 keeps proper pairs, -F 1804 removes unmapped (4), mate unmapped (8), secondary (256), QC-fail (512) and duplicates (1024). After filtering single reads, some pairs have lost a mate; samtools fixmate on name-grouped reads (collate) clears their proper-pair flag, so the second view -f 2 drops them. samtools markdup needs the MC/ms tags that fixmate -m adds and coordinate-sorted input; -r removes duplicates, -s prints statistics.

Hint 4 — MACS3 (C4)

-f BAMPE uses whole fragments from a paired-end BAM. For a BED of insertions, --nomodel --shift -75 --extsize 150 makes a 150-bp window centred on each line’s 5’ end (the start, for strand +). --keep-dup all because duplicates were removed already. -g is the effective genome size. Output formats are described under callpeak outputs; the narrowPeak columns in Genomic intervals.

Hint 5 — overlaps and FRiP (B5, B6, C5, C6)

Count each interval once with bedtools intersect -u. The two directions of an overlap differ because one peak can overlap several in the other set. FRiP is defined in the ENCODE ATAC-seq standards. IDR: Li et al. 2011 and the idr package.

Hint 6 — fixed-width peaks (B7, R2)

Sort by score; take the best; remove everything that overlaps it (findOverlaps of the set with itself); continue with the best peak not yet removed. resize(x, 500, fix = "center") makes the windows. Corces et al. 2018 (Methods, “peak calling”) describe the procedure.

Hint 7 — annotation (R3)

distanceToNearest gives 0 for overlaps. Build TSSs as in Genomic intervals (R2) and restrict to chr21, since our peaks are only there.

Solutions

Solutions — by hand

B1. (a) Read 1: BED [10000, 10050); read 2: BED [10230, 10280). Fragment [10000, 10280), TLEN 280. (b) \(10000 + 4 = 10004\) → chr21 10004 10005; \(10280 - 5 = 10275\) → chr21 10275 10276; 271 bp apart. (c) BED [20000, 20030), insertion \(20030 - 5 = 20025\). The peak sits in a region of high background; against its neighbourhood it is only weakly significant, and would not pass a cutoff of \(q < 0.01\) after testing millions of positions. (d) The maximum is conservative: if any scale shows high background (a broad open domain, a copy-number gain, a mappability artefact), the candidate must exceed it. A mean would let local biases through.

B4. (a) \(10^6 \times 10^{-4} = 100\). (b) About \(100 / 2000 = 0.05\), i.e. q ≈ 0.05: about 5% of these windows are expected to be noise. (c) At \(p < 0.01\) you expect \(10^4\) false windows on chr21 alone, so most of the peaks are noise. IDR needs exactly that: a long ranked list including noise, so that it can see where replicate agreement breaks down.

B5. (a) \(1\,050\,000 / 3\,100\,000 = 0.34\): passes (> 0.3). (b) No: the library is the same; more lenient peaks cover more of the genome and catch more insertions, so FRiP rises. FRiP is only comparable between samples with the same peak-calling settings, or when counted in one fixed reference peak set.

B6. (a) 3200/4000 = 80% of replicate 1, 3350/5000 = 67% of replicate 2. They differ because overlap is not one-to-one: one wide peak in one set can overlap two narrow peaks in the other, and the sets have different sizes. (b) 3900/8000 = 49%: the 4000 extra peaks are weaker and mostly noise or real but weak sites below replicate 2’s threshold, so the fraction falls. The overlap fraction measures the threshold as much as the data. (c) IDR ranks the peaks of both replicates (called leniently) and fits a mixture of a reproducible component, in which ranks are correlated, and an irreproducible one, in which they are not. Each peak gets the probability of being irreproducible, and the cutoff is set on that (e.g. IDR ≤ 0.05), independent of the original p-value thresholds.

(d) Equal widths make counts comparable between regions and samples (no length effect); centring on the summit keeps the most informative part; overlapping peaks of different samples do not chain into long regions; adding samples does not change the existing regions’ boundaries.

Suggest fixed-width summit-centred peaks with iterative overlap removal (or at least requiring presence in several samples and splitting long regions).

Solutions — R

R1. (a) 1739 and 1909 lenient peaks. Of replicate 1’s top 1000, 97.3% are found in replicate 2; top 1250: 93.8%; top 1500: 88.5%; all 1739: 82.2%; the top 750 are 99.3% reproduced. (b) The curve is flat to about 1000 peaks and drops faster after about 1300–1500, similar to the 1262 peaks at q < 0.01, and below ENCODE’s 1784 IDR peaks on chr21 (made from more reads, including multi-mappers). (c) Spearman correlation 0.82 among matched peaks. The correlation is computed over all matched peaks, including the many weak ones whose scores are mostly noise; agreement among the strongest peaks is near perfect. This is why IDR models the ranks as a mixture rather than using one correlation.

R2. (a) MACS3 writes the summit as an offset from the 0-based start; rtracklayer has added 1 to the start, so start(p) + p$peak is the 1-based position of the summit base (Genomic intervals, R1b). (b) 1262 and 1323 peaks become 1116 and 1123 non-overlapping 500-bp peaks per replicate (nearby peaks whose 500-bp windows overlap are resolved); the joint consensus has 1286 peaks, all 500 bp wide (table(width(cons))), and 958 of them overlap a peak from both replicates. The merged set (C7) has 1442 regions of variable width (median 289 bp, up to 1820 bp). (c) −log10 p grows with sequencing depth: a deeper sample would win every overlap. Dividing by the sample’s total score puts the samples on the same scale before they compete. (d) 0: no two consensus peaks overlap.

R3. (a) 17.3% of the 958 reproducible consensus peaks are within 1 kb of a protein-coding TSS; 25.8% of ENCODE’s chr21 peaks. (b) Strongest decile 70%, second 25%, the rest 5–15%: the strongest accessible sites are promoters, and the large majority of weaker peaks are distal: candidate enhancers, CTCF sites and other regulatory elements, plus some noise. (c) 59% of the 602 distinct protein-coding TSS positions on chr21 have a consensus peak within 1 kb. Plausible: a B-cell line expresses perhaps half to two thirds of protein-coding genes, and the rest have closed promoters; alternative TSSs of one gene are also counted separately. The R part runs in under a minute.

Where this leads

  • Differential accessibility starts from a count matrix over fixed-width 500-bp peaks like the consensus you built in R2 (the Corces et al. 2016 haematopoiesis data) and tests it with DESeq2.
  • Motifs, motif enrichment and TF footprinting uses the summits and the base-resolution insertion sites: there the +4/−5 shift matters.
  • Peak calling (ChIP-seq) uses the same MACS3 model with an input control, where the 1-kb and 5-kb windows come from the control.
