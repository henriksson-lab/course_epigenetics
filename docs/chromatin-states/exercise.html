Exercise: Combining marks into chromatin states

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Binarise read counts in 200-bp bins with a Poisson threshold, by hand and with ChromHMM.
  • Compute emission probabilities, and run the forward and Viterbi algorithms by hand on a small hidden Markov model.
  • Learn a chromatin-state model for GM12878 chr21 with ChromHMM, and name its states from the emission matrix and from enrichments at TSSs, genes and CpG islands.
  • Compare your segmentation with the published Roadmap 18-state annotation of the same cell line, in R.
  • Decode a chromatin-state sequence yourself in R with the learned parameters, and judge claims based on chromatin-state annotations.

Background

Bins and binarisation. The genome is cut into 200-bp bins. For each mark, a bin is called 1 if its read count \(k\) is improbably high under a Poisson background: \(P(X \ge k \mid \lambda) < 10^{-4}\). Without a control, λ is the mark’s mean count per bin; with an input control, ChromHMM multiplies it by the local input enrichment (input reads in the bin ± 5 bins, plus a pseudocount, relative to the input’s mean).

The HMM. Each bin has a hidden state \(s_t\). The model has initial probabilities \(\pi_s\), transition probabilities \(a_{rs} = P(s_{t+1} = s \mid s_t = r)\), and for each state and mark an emission probability \(e_{sm} = P(\text{mark } m \text{ present} \mid s)\). Marks are independent given the state:

\[P(o_t \mid s) = \prod_m e_{sm}^{\,o_{tm}} (1 - e_{sm})^{1 - o_{tm}}\]

Forward (probability of the data): \(\alpha_1(s) = \pi_s P(o_1 \mid s)\); \(\alpha_t(s) = \big[\sum_r \alpha_{t-1}(r)\, a_{rs}\big] P(o_t \mid s)\); \(P(o_{1..T}) = \sum_s \alpha_T(s)\).

Viterbi (best state path): the same with max instead of sum, \(\delta_t(s) = \max_r [\delta_{t-1}(r)\, a_{rs}]\, P(o_t \mid s)\); remember the best \(r\) and trace back from the best final state.

Fold enrichment of state \(s\) for an annotation \(A\) = (fraction of \(A\)’s bins that are in \(s\)) / (fraction of all bins that are in \(s\)).

Data

ENCODE ChIP-seq of GM12878 (Bernstein lab, GRCh38, filtered alignments, replicates 1 and 2 merged, cut to chr21) for the six marks of the Roadmap 18-state model, H3K4me3 (ENCSR000AKA), H3K27ac (ENCSR000AKC), H3K4me1 (ENCSR000AKF), H3K36me3 (ENCSR000AKE), H3K27me3 (ENCSR000AKD), H3K9me3 (ENCSR000AOX), and the input (ENCSR000AKJ). These are the experiments behind the fold-change bigWigs of What is epigenetics?. For comparison: the Roadmap 18-state segmentation of GM12878 (epigenome E116, lifted to hg38).

Honest simplifications: one chromosome; one cell type (no concatenated model); six marks; and our model is learned from chr21 alone (about 234 000 bins), whereas the Roadmap model was learned jointly on many epigenomes and the whole genome. The Roadmap annotation was made on hg19 and lifted over to hg38.

By hand

Do these on paper.

B1. Binarisation. On chr21 (46.7 Mb) a mark has 600 000 reads.

  1. How many 200-bp bins does chr21 have? What is the mean number of reads per bin, λ?

  2. Using the table, what is the smallest count \(k^*\) with \(P(X \ge k^*) < 10^{-4}\)?

\(k\) 9 10 11 12
\(P(X \ge k \mid 2.57)\) 0.0014 3.4 × 10-4 7.8 × 10-5 1.6 × 10-5

  1. In a bin, the input has twice its average density. What is λ there? Will the threshold go up or down, and why is that sensible?

  2. Another mark was sequenced half as deeply. Without changing anything else, what happens to the fraction of bins called 1? Why does this matter for a chromatin-state model?

B2. Emissions. A learned model has these emission probabilities:

state H3K4me3 H3K27ac H3K4me1 H3K36me3 H3K27me3 H3K9me3
1 0.92 0.85 0.30 0.03 0.02 0.01
2 0.05 0.70 0.92 0.04 0.01 0.01
3 0.01 0.02 0.05 0.75 0.01 0.04
4 0.40 0.05 0.55 0.02 0.85 0.02
5 0.01 0.01 0.02 0.03 0.02 0.02

  1. Name each state.

  2. Compute \(P(o \mid s)\) for states 1 and 2 for a bin with H3K27ac and H3K4me1 present and all others absent. Which state explains it better, and by how much?

  3. A bin has H3K4me3, H3K4me1 and H3K27me3 present. Without computing everything, which state wins? Which single factor makes the biggest difference against state 1?

B3. Forward algorithm. A toy HMM has states A (active) and Q (quiet), one mark, \(\pi\) = (0.5, 0.5), \(P(\text{mark} \mid A) = 0.8\), \(P(\text{mark} \mid Q) = 0.1\), \(P(\text{stay}) = 0.9\), \(P(\text{switch}) = 0.1\). The observed sequence is 1, 0, 1.

  1. Compute \(\alpha_1\), \(\alpha_2\) and \(\alpha_3\) for both states.

  2. What is \(P(1, 0, 1)\)?

  3. What is the posterior probability that bin 3 is in state A, given all three observations?

B4. Viterbi. For the same model and sequence 1, 0, 1:

  1. Compute \(\delta_t(A)\) and \(\delta_t(Q)\) for \(t\) = 1, 2, 3, and the back-pointers.

  2. What is the most probable state path, and its probability? Check it against the brute-force list of all eight paths (you only need to compute the two or three best candidates).

  3. Change \(P(\text{stay})\) to 0.6. Does the path for 1, 0, 1 change? What does \(P(\text{stay})\) control?

B5. Enrichment. On chr21 there are 233 549 bins. A state covers 1 120 bins; 640 bins overlap a CpG island, 120 of which are in that state.

  1. Compute the fold enrichment of the state for CpG islands.

  2. What fraction of the state’s bins are CpG-island bins? Why can a state be highly enriched at CpG islands and still mostly lie outside them?

B6. Read the transition matrix. In a learned 8-state model, the transition probability from “TSS flank” to “active TSS” is 0.13, from “TSS flank” to “transcribed” 0.08, and from “quiescent” to “quiescent” 0.999. (a) What is the expected length of a run of quiescent bins, in kb? (b) What do the off-diagonal entries say about the order of elements along a gene?

B7. How many states? A colleague learns models with 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 states. The log-likelihood increases every time, and she chooses 25. What is wrong with the argument, and what would you do instead?

B8. Judge the claims. (a) “Variant rs… lies in an EnhA state in E116, so it disrupts the enhancer of the nearest gene.” (b) “We compared the chromatin states of cell type X (our data, 10 M reads per mark) with Roadmap’s state map of cell type Y and found 3000 regions that switch from enhancer to quiescent.” For each, say what has and has not been shown.

Explore: transitions versus emissions

The demo is a small ChromHMM-style model: three hidden states, TSS (active promoter), Tx (transcribed gene body) and Pc (Polycomb-repressed), along 150 bins of 200 bp. Each state emits four binary marks, independently given the state, with the probabilities in the emission table (presets or sliders). The true self-transition is the probability that the next bin stays in the same state (the rest is split evenly between the other two states); it is used to generate the simulated sequence: first the hidden states, then the marks of each bin (top four rows). The model then forgets the true states and decodes the marks three ways, all with the same emissions: Viterbi (the single most probable path), the posterior P(state | all marks) from the forward–backward algorithm (stacked colours; the most probable state per bin is what ChromHMM reports), and emissions only (each bin gets the state that best explains its own marks). The decoder has its own decoder self-transition; at 1/3 it has no preference for staying, and Viterbi becomes the same as emissions only. Black ticks under a strip mark wrongly decoded bins. New sequence draws new random numbers; any other change re-simulates with the same random numbers.

E1. With the default settings (clear marks, both self-transitions 0.9), how many bins does each method decode correctly, and how many segments does each path have? Look at the emissions-only strip inside the long transcribed region (5–15 kb): which state does it give to bins with no mark at all? Compute P(no marks | state) for the three states from the emission table to explain it, and say why Viterbi does not do the same.

E2. Press 1/3 (emissions only), then move the decoder self-transition to 0.6, 0.9 and 0.99. For each, note the Viterbi accuracy, the number of Viterbi segments and log P(marks | decoder). At which value is log P highest? Try 0.925 too. What does this suggest about how the transition probabilities can be learned?

E3. Choose noisy marks (decoder 0.9). Compare the three methods. Then set the decoder to 1/3 and to 0.99. When do transitions matter most, and what goes wrong when the decoder is too sticky?

E4. Choose clear marks again and set the true self-transition to 0.6 (short elements), keeping the decoder at 0.9. Compare Viterbi with emissions only. Then press = true (decoder 0.6). Which decoder setting fits these data better, by accuracy and by log P?

Solutions

E1. Correct: Viterbi 96%, posterior 98%, emissions only 79%; segments: true 12, Viterbi 10, posterior 12, emissions only 55. Unmarked bins in the gene body are called Pc by emissions only: P(no marks | Pc) = 0.90 × 0.98 × 0.97 × 0.35 = 0.299, P(no marks | Tx) = 0.95 × 0.90 × 0.30 × 0.97 = 0.249, P(no marks | TSS) = 0.15 × 0.25 × 0.95 × 0.95 = 0.034. A bin without H3K36me3 is slightly better explained by Pc, whose only common mark (H3K27me3) is also absent. Viterbi keeps such bins in Tx: switching Tx → Pc → Tx costs two transition factors of (1 − 0.9)/2 = 0.05, against staying (0.9 × 0.9), which is far more than the 0.299/0.249 = 1.2-fold gain in the emission. This is the toy example of the lecture with real-looking marks: one missed mark does not split an element.

E2.

decoder self-transition Viterbi correct Viterbi segments log P
1/3 79% 55 −305.5
0.6 95% 18 −264.8
0.9 96% 10 −237.7
0.925 96% 10 −237.3
0.99 97% 9 −246.8

At 1/3, Viterbi is identical to emissions only (55 segments). Raising the self-transition smooths the path. Log P is highest near the true value: the maximum is at about 0.925, the fraction of steps that stay in this particular sequence (138 of 149, 12 segments). Choosing the transitions that make the observed marks most probable recovers how long the elements really are: that is what Baum–Welch does when it learns the transition matrix.

E3. With noisy marks (decoder 0.9): Viterbi 85% (8 segments), posterior 89% (12), emissions only 49% (84 segments). Single bins are now unreliable, so the neighbours carry most of the information: transitions matter most when the emissions are weak. At decoder 1/3 all methods drop to 49%. At 0.99 the decoder is too sticky: Viterbi merges real elements into 3 segments and falls to 70% (log P −326.8, worse than −321.6 at 0.9).

E4. With true self-transition 0.6 the sequence has 70 segments. A decoder at 0.9 over-smooths: Viterbi 82% (43 segments), worse than emissions only (87%, 76 segments). With = true (0.6), Viterbi reaches 90% (57 segments), and log P rises from −347.6 to −313.2. The transition matrix must describe the data: if it assumes longer elements than exist, the HMM erases short ones (such as a short enhancer between two other states). ChromHMM learns the transitions from the data for this reason.

R4 — Viterbi with the learned model

viterbi <- function(O, em, tr, init) {          # O: bins x marks (0/1); em: states x marks
  E <- as.matrix(em[, colnames(O)]); A <- log(as.matrix(tr)); K <- nrow(E); n <- nrow(O)
  le <- O %*% t(log(E)) + (1 - O) %*% t(log(1 - E))       # log P(o_t | s), n x K
  d <- matrix(0, n, K); bp <- matrix(0L, n, K)
  d[1, ] <- log(init) + le[1, ]
  for (t in 2:n) {
    cand <- d[t - 1, ] + A                                 # K x K: from r (rows) to s (cols)
    bp[t, ] <- apply(cand, 2, which.max)
    d[t, ] <- cand[cbind(bp[t, ], 1:K)] + le[t, ]
  }
  path <- integer(n); path[n] <- which.max(d[n, ])
  for (t in n:2) path[t - 1] <- bp[t, path[t]]
  path
}
O <- as.matrix(b[, colnames(em)])
init <- rep(1 / nrow(em), nrow(em))
v <- viterbi(O, em, tr, init)
ours <- as.integer(sub("E", "", bins$ours))
mean(v == ours)                                           # agreement with ChromHMM's posterior decoding
# without transitions: assign each bin its most likely state from the emissions alone
le <- O %*% t(log(as.matrix(em))) + (1 - O) %*% t(log(1 - as.matrix(em)))
nt <- apply(le, 1, which.max)
mean(nt == ours); c(segments_viterbi = sum(diff(v) != 0) + 1, segments_no_transitions = sum(diff(nt) != 0) + 1)

Hints

Hint 1 — Poisson thresholds (B1, C1)

\(\lambda\) = reads / bins; with a control, λ is scaled by the local input enrichment. The ChromHMM manual describes BinarizeBam/BinarizeBed: options -b (bin size), -p (Poisson threshold, default 10-4), -n (read shift, default 100 bp), -u and -w (pseudocount and window of the control). The cell-mark file table has four columns: cell type, mark, read file, control file.

Hint 2 — HMM algorithms (B3, B4, R4)

Write the forward and Viterbi recursions as a table with one column per bin and one row per state. Work with probabilities on paper; in code, use logarithms (products of many small numbers underflow). Rabiner’s tutorial on HMMs (Proc IEEE 1989) is the classic reference; Ernst & Kellis 2012 and the ChromHMM manual describe the model ChromHMM uses (multivariate Bernoulli emissions).

Hint 3 — LearnModel and its outputs (C2, C3)

LearnModel [options] inputdir outputdir numstates assembly; -l restricts output to the chromosomes in a file, -p sets the number of processors, -noautoopen stops it from opening a browser. Outputs are described in the ChromHMM manual: emissions_K.txt, transitions_K.txt, *_segments.bed (chromosome, start, end, state), *_overlap.txt (fold enrichments), *_neighborhood.txt. Ernst & Kellis 2017 (Nat Protoc) discusses choosing the number of states.

Hint 4 — Roadmap states (C4, R2)

The 18-state model’s states, emissions and colours are described on the Roadmap chromatin-state page and in the Roadmap 2015 paper (Fig. 4). bedtools intersect -wo reports the number of overlapping bases as the last column. In R, findOverlaps(..., select = "first") and tile().

B1. (a) 46 709 983 / 200 ≈ 233 550 bins; λ = 600 000 / 233 550 = 2.57. (b) \(k^* = 11\): \(P(X \ge 11) = 7.8 \times 10^{-5} < 10^{-4}\), while \(P(X \ge 10) = 3.4 \times 10^{-4}\) is still above. (c) λ = 5.14; the threshold rises (to 16 reads). A region with more input reads (an amplification, a repeat with mappability problems, an open region that shears easily) gives more ChIP reads without enrichment; a higher threshold avoids calling the mark there. (d) λ halves, the threshold falls a little, but the counts at true sites halve too, so fewer bins pass: the mark is called present in fewer bins (low sensitivity), most for broad, weakly enriched marks (H3K36me3, H3K27me3, H3K9me3). The model then learns low emission probabilities for that mark, and states defined by it become weak or merge with “quiescent”. Unequal depth between marks or between cell types changes the states.

B6. (a) The run length is geometric with mean 1 / (1 − 0.999) = 1000 bins = 200 kb. B7. Models with more states contain the smaller ones as special cases, so the likelihood always increases; it cannot choose K. Instead: look for the smallest model whose states are biologically distinct and interpretable (enrichments), check that states are reproducible (e.g. between replicates or chromosomes), and compare models with ChromHMM’s CompareModels (how well each state of a big model is represented in smaller ones). The Roadmap consortium chose 15 states for 5 marks and 18 for 6 marks this way.

B8. (a) Shown: in GM12878, the chromatin at the variant looks like an active enhancer (H3K4me1 + H3K27ac). Not shown: that it acts as an enhancer, that its target is the nearest gene, that the variant changes it, that GM12878 is the relevant cell type, or that this variant (and not one in LD) is causal. (b) Not shown: that the differences are biological. Different labs, antibodies, depths (10 M vs Roadmap’s subsampled depth), processing and possibly marks change binarisation and states. A fair comparison needs the same marks processed the same way at the same depth, ideally in one concatenated model, with replicates.

Where this leads

  • Epigenomics and genetic variation tests whether trait-associated variants are enriched in chromatin states of particular cell types, with proper background models.
  • Linking regulatory elements to genes asks which gene an enhancer state regulates.
  • What people try to predict, and how to tell if it works includes imputing missing marks, which is how the Roadmap 25-state model was made.
