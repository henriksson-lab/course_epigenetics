Exercise: 3D genome organisation

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Read a Hi-C contact map: find the domains, loops and compartments in it, and say which loci a pixel connects.
  • Predict, on paper, how deleting or inverting a CTCF site, or removing CTCF or cohesin, changes a map.
  • Choose an assay from the 3C family for a given question, and judge claims about enhancer–promoter contact and disease.
  • Open a real Hi-C matrix (.cool) on the command line and in Python, measure how much of the genome’s contacts stay within chromosomes, and look at domains and loops in GM12878.

Background

Contact map. The genome is cut into bins (1 Mb to 1 kb). Pixel \((i, j)\) counts the read pairs that join bin \(i\) to bin \(j\), summed over millions of cells; the matrix is symmetric. “Balanced” maps have been corrected so that every bin has the same total (Hi-C: from reads to contact matrices).

Distance decay. The average contact frequency at genomic distance \(s\), \(P(s)\), falls roughly as \(s^{-1}\) between about 100 kb and a few Mb. Enrichment is measured against it: O/E = observed / expected at the same distance.

Features. Compartments: a checkerboard of A (active) and B (inactive) chromatin. Domains (TADs): squares on the diagonal, 0.1–1 Mb. Loops: dots off the diagonal, mostly between convergent CTCF sites (→ … ←), made by cohesin loop extrusion.

Depletion. Without CTCF, most domains and loops are lost and compartments stay; without cohesin, all loop domains are lost and compartments get stronger; without WAPL, loops get longer.

By hand

B1. Read a map. A balanced contact map of eight 50-kb bins (only the upper triangle is shown; the matrix is symmetric):

        1     2     3     4     5     6     7     8
1     100    40    22    15     4     3     3     2
2           100    42    20     5     4     3     3
3                 100    41     6     4     3     2
4                       100     7     5     4     3
5                             100    45    25    18
6                                   100    43    24
7                                         100    44
8                                               100

  1. Where are the domains (TADs)? Where is the boundary between them?

  2. Pixel (1, 4) is 15, pixel (4, 7) is 4. Both are 150 kb apart. Why so different?

  3. Pixel (5, 8) = 18 is the highest of all pixels 150 kb from the diagonal. Is it a loop? What would make you call it one?

B2. Distance decay. Take \(P(s) \propto s^{-1}\).

  1. How much less often do two loci 1 Mb apart contact each other than two loci 100 kb apart? And 10 Mb apart?

  2. A pixel 500 kb from the diagonal has 60 contacts. The average of all pixels 500 kb from the diagonal on that chromosome is 20. What is \(O/E\)? Why is the raw count alone not enough to say a contact is special?

  3. The diagonal of every Hi-C map is bright. Is that biology?

B3. CTCF orientation. Four CTCF sites lie along a chromosome (motif orientation in brackets). A gene promoter lies at 200 kb and an enhancer at 600 kb:

site            A     B     C     D
position (kb)  100   300   450   700
orientation     →     ←     →     ←
promoter at 200 kb, enhancer at 600 kb

  1. Cohesin can be loaded anywhere. Which loops do you expect? Which are the main domains, and where is the boundary between them?

  2. Are the promoter and the enhancer in the same domain? Do you expect the enhancer to activate the gene strongly?

  3. Site C is deleted. What changes? (d) Instead, site C is inverted (→ becomes ←). What changes?

B4. Remove CTCF, remove cohesin. Sketch, for one chromosome arm, a control Hi-C map with two domains, a loop and a checkerboard.

  1. Sketch it after acute CTCF depletion, and after acute cohesin depletion. Which features remain in each?

  2. A paper concludes: “CTCF depletion lost most TADs but changed expression of only ~200 genes, so 3D organisation is irrelevant for gene regulation.” Give three reasons this conclusion is too strong.

B5. Territories. A toy genome has three chromosomes of 100, 50 and 50 Mb.

  1. If contact partners were picked at random from the whole genome, what fraction of contacts would be within a chromosome?

  2. A Hi-C library has 80% of contacts within chromosomes. What does that say about the nucleus? A second library from the same cells has 45%. What might have gone wrong?

B6. One cell versus millions. Two loci are 300 kb apart. DNA-FISH in 200 cells finds them within 200 nm of each other in 20 cells (10%) and more than 600 nm apart in most others. Hi-C shows a strong dot between them.

  1. Are the two results in conflict?

  2. A student writes “the enhancer and promoter are in a stable loop”. Rewrite the sentence so it matches the data.

B7. Choose the assay. For each question, name the most suitable method from the 3C family (or imaging) and say why:

  1. Does one candidate enhancer contact one promoter, in 30 patient samples, cheaply?
  2. All contacts of 2 000 gene promoters at high resolution, to link GWAS variants to genes.
  3. Compartments in a new cell type, genome-wide.
  4. Loops anchored by cohesin, genome-wide, with a limited sequencing budget.
  5. Enhancer–promoter contacts at nucleosome resolution.
  6. How variable a domain’s shape is from cell to cell.

B8. A patient’s structural variant. A child with a limb malformation carries a 250-kb deletion. Before the deletion, the region looks like this:

[ domain 1: gene X (expressed in brain) ] | CTCF boundary | [ domain 2: limb enhancers, gene Y (expressed in limb) ]
                                        deletion: ─────────────── (removes the boundary and gene Y)

  1. Predict what happens to gene X in the developing limb, and why.

  2. Which data would you collect to test your prediction, in patient cells or in a mouse model?

  3. A second patient has a deletion of the same size inside domain 2 that does not touch the boundary. Would you expect the same phenotype?

In Python

Start Python (or a Jupyter notebook) in ~/epi/genome3d:

import numpy as np, pandas as pd, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import LogNorm
import cooler, cooltools
D = "/path/to/course/data"
MC = f"{D}/gm12878_hic_chr19_chr21.mcool::resolutions/"

P1 — The whole genome

g = cooler.Cooler(f"{D}/gm12878_hic_genome_1mb.cool")
print(g.chromnames[:5], len(g.bins()[:]), g.info["sum"])
A = g.matrix(balance=True)[:]
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 6))
plt.imshow(A, cmap="Reds", norm=LogNorm(vmin=np.nanpercentile(A[A > 0], 20), vmax=np.nanpercentile(A, 99.5)))
plt.colorbar(label="balanced contacts"); plt.title("GM12878, 1 Mb"); plt.savefig("p1_genome.png", dpi=120)

pix = g.pixels(join=True)[:]
cis = {c: pix["count"][((pix.chrom1 == c) | (pix.chrom2 == c)) & (pix.chrom1 == pix.chrom2)].sum()
          / pix["count"][(pix.chrom1 == c) | (pix.chrom2 == c)].sum() for c in g.chromnames}
cis = pd.Series(cis).round(3)
print(cis.sort_values())

  1. Do the numbers agree with C2?

  2. Which chromosomes have the lowest and highest cis fractions? Relate this to their size and gene density (chr17, chr19 and chr22 are small and gene-rich; chr18 is small and gene-poor). In the map, which off-diagonal blocks are brightest?

for chrom in ["chr19", "chr21"]:

  1. Describe the checkerboard on chr19. Is it visible on chr21?

  2. The first 14 Mb of the chr21 plot are empty (white). Why? (Hint: what is the short arm of chr21 made of?)

P3 — Distance decay

c = cooler.Cooler(MC + "10000")
view = pd.DataFrame({"chrom": ["chr19", "chr21"], "start": 0,
                     "end": [c.chromsizes["chr19"], c.chromsizes["chr21"]], "name": ["chr19", "chr21"]})
exp = cooltools.expected_cis(c, view_df=view, nproc=2)
exp["s"] = exp["dist"] * 10000
for chrom in ["chr19", "chr21"]:
    e = exp[(exp.region1 == chrom) & (exp.dist >= 2)]
    plt.loglog(e.s, e["balanced.avg"], label=chrom)
    sel = (e.s >= 1e5) & (e.s <= 1e6)
    print(chrom, "slope 100 kb - 1 Mb:", round(np.polyfit(np.log10(e.s[sel]), np.log10(e["balanced.avg"][sel]), 1)[0], 2))
plt.xlabel("distance (bp)"); plt.ylabel("P(s)"); plt.legend(); plt.savefig("p3_ps.png", dpi=120)
e = exp[exp.region1 == "chr21"].set_index("s")["balanced.avg"]
print("P(100 kb) / P(1 Mb) on chr21:", round(e[100000] / e[1000000], 1))

  1. What slopes do you find? Compare with B2.

  2. How many times more often do loci 100 kb apart contact than loci 1 Mb apart on chr21? Compare with the \(s^{-1}\) prediction.

P4 — Domains and loops in one window

res, chrom, s0, s1 = 10000, "chr21", 24_750_000, 26_250_000
c = cooler.Cooler(MC + str(res))
m = c.matrix(balance=True).fetch(f"{chrom}:{s0}-{s1}")

ext = np.array([s0, s1, s1, s0]) / 1e6
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 6.5))
plt.imshow(m, cmap="Reds", extent=ext, norm=LogNorm(vmin=np.nanpercentile(m[m > 0], 30), vmax=np.nanpercentile(m, 99.7)))
plt.scatter((lp[4] + lp[5]) / 2e6, (lp[1] + lp[2]) / 2e6, s=150, facecolors="none", edgecolors="blue")
for _, d in dm.iterrows():

    plt.plot([a, b, b], [a, a, b], color="black", lw=1, ls="--")
plt.title(f"{chrom}:{s0:,}-{s1:,}, {res // 1000} kb"); plt.savefig("p4_window.png", dpi=120)

  2. Pick one loop and read off its two anchor positions. Use the CTCF peak file (or a genome browser with the CTCF track) to check whether both anchors have a CTCF peak.

  3. Change res to 5000 and to 50000. What can you see at each resolution, and what do you lose?

Explore: loop extrusion

The demo simulates loop extrusion on 1 Mb of chromosome, cut into 200 bins of 5 kb. Cohesin complexes are loaded at random positions (loads per 100 steps), extrude a loop by moving both legs outward by one bin per time step, and are released after a random time (mean lifetime, in steps). A leg stops at a CTCF site whose motif points into the loop (▶ stops legs moving left, ◀ stops legs moving right) with the CTCF stop probability; a site in the other orientation is passed. Two legs cannot pass each other. The track under the map shows the loops of one cell (filled circle: leg stopped at CTCF). The triangle above is the contact map averaged over 1 200 snapshots (30 cells × 40 time points), in the same coordinates as the track. In each snapshot two bins contact each other with frequency 1/d, where d is their distance along the folded chain: the two legs of a cohesin hold the chain together, so a loop is a shortcut. Without cohesin, d is the genomic distance and the map is pure distance decay, P(s) = s−1 (the dashed line in the P(s) plot). Click the chain to add a ▶ site, turn ▶ into ◀, or remove a ◀ site; click the map to read one pixel; show obs/exp divides every pixel by the mean of its diagonal. The readout lists the strongest observed/expected pixels between pairs of CTCF sites. Simulated data: every change restarts the simulation from the same seed, so the same settings always give the same numbers.

E1. Start with Two domains + stripe. Which two pairs of CTCF sites give the strongest dots, how are their motifs oriented, and how strong are they (obs/exp)? Where are the two domains? The third pair in the list, 150 kb ▶ – 410 kb ▶, is in tandem: one cohesin cannot be stopped by both sites. Why does its pixel still light up? (Hint: where is 410 kb relative to 400 kb?)

E2. Press Flip one site: the site at 400 kb now points ▶. What happens to the 150–400 kb dot, and which new pair of sites makes a dot? Compare with the CTCF inversion experiment of the lecture.

E3. Press Delete one site (the 400 kb site is gone), then No CTCF, then No cohesin. For each, describe the dots and domains, and note the readout “P(s) at 250 kb”. Which depletion experiment of the lecture does each of the last two mimic, and what does P(s) tell you that the dots do not?

E4. Go back to Two domains + stripe. Lower the CTCF stop probability to 0.5, then to 0. Then set it to 0.9 again and tick one-sided extrusion. What happens to the dots, to the fraction of legs stopped at CTCF, and to the mean loop size? Where do you now see stripes?

Solutions

E1. The two strongest pixels are both convergent pairs: 150 kb ▶ – 400 kb ◀ (obs/exp 8.6) and 410 kb ▶ – 650 kb ◀ (7.4). They are the apexes of two triangles, the domains 150–400 kb and 410–650 kb; inside each, contacts are raised along the edges (the anchors keep reeling in DNA) and the domains are separated by the divergent pair at 400/410 kb. The tandem pair 150 ▶ – 410 ▶ reaches 4.1 because 410 kb is only two bins from 400 kb: in a cell where the 150–400 loop is held, 150 kb sits next to 400 kb and therefore also close to 410 kb. Neighbouring loop anchors cluster; this is also seen in real maps. (The model has 7.9 cohesins per cell, one per 127 kb, and 21% of legs stopped at CTCF.)

E2. The 150–400 kb pixel drops from 8.6 to 3.4 and leaves the top of the list. Legs coming from 150 kb now pass the site at 400 kb (it points away from them) and the next site that stops them is 650 kb ◀: a new dot appears at 150 ▶ – 650 ◀ (4.5, up from 3.2), the apex of a larger domain. This is the inversion experiment: the A–B loop is lost and an A–C loop forms with the next site in the right orientation. What remains at 150–400 kb comes from cohesins stacked against each other near 400/410 kb.

E3. Delete one site: the two domains merge; the strongest pixels are 410–650 kb (5.3) and 150–650 kb (3.9). No CTCF: no dots and no domains (the list is empty; any pixel you click is close to 1), but P(s) at 250 kb is still 2.7× the no-cohesin line: cohesin still extrudes loops, only at random places. This is the CTCF degron (Nora et al. 2017): loops and domains go, extrusion goes on. No cohesin: every pixel has obs/exp 1 and P(s) is exactly s−1 (1.0×): the cohesin degron (Rao et al. 2017), all loop domains gone. With cohesin, P(s) at 250 kb is 3.1× the no-cohesin value (default). P(s) shows whether loops are being extruded at all, even when no single position stands out. (Compartments are not part of this model.)

E4. With stop probability 0.5, the dots weaken (410–650 kb 4.5, 150–400 kb 3.9) and 14% of the legs are stopped (21% at 0.9). At 0 nothing is stopped (0%), the dots are gone (≤ 1.1) and the domains disappear, although cohesin still makes loops. With one-sided extrusion (stop probability 0.9) only one leg moves, and a dot needs a cohesin that was loaded right at one anchor: the dots almost vanish (150–400 kb 1.9, 410–650 kb 1.5), 10% of the legs are stopped and the mean loop size halves (73 kb, against 136 kb). What remains are stripes: lines running from the CTCF sites along the edges of the domains, e.g. from the ▶ site at 800 kb towards the upper right (click the pixel 800 kb – 900 kb: about 1.4 one-sided against 1.1 two-sided). Purely one-sided extrusion struggles to make dots between convergent sites; this is one argument that cohesin extrudes in both directions, at least on average.

Hints

Hint 1 — reading maps and distance decay (B1, B2)

A domain is a block of high values near the diagonal with a sharp drop outside it. Compare pixels at the same distance from the diagonal (the same off-diagonal): only those are comparable. \(P(s) \propto s^{-1}\) means \(P(s_1)/P(s_2) = s_2/s_1\). For the bright diagonal, think of what the bin of a pixel \((i, i)\) contains. Background: Lieberman-Aiden et al. 2009, Lajoie et al. 2015.

Hint 2 — CTCF and extrusion (B3, B4)

Cohesin extrudes in both directions and stops at a CTCF site whose motif points towards it, i.e. into the loop: a → site on the left, a ← site on the right. A loop needs a → site to the left of a ← site with no intervening site of the right orientation that would stop cohesin first (in most cells). Fudenberg et al. 2016, Sanborn et al. 2015, Nora et al. 2017, Rao et al. 2017.

Hint 3 — assays, imaging and disease (B5–B8)

The expected cis fraction for random partners is \(\sum_k L_k^2 / (\sum_k L_k)^2\). For assays, ask: how many viewpoints, genome-wide or targeted, how deep, is a protein involved, single cells or population? Reviews: Lajoie et al. 2015; single cells: Bintu et al. 2018; disease: Lupiáñez et al. 2015.

Hint 4 — command line (C1–C4)

cooler info, cooler ls, cooler dump: --join writes chromosome names and positions instead of bin IDs, -r selects a region, --balanced adds the balanced value. A .mcool holds several resolutions; address one as file.mcool::resolutions/10000. BEDPE has two intervals per line; columns 1–3 and 4–6 are the two loop anchors. bedtools shuffle with -chrom keeps each interval on its chromosome.

Hint 5 — Python (P1–P4)

cooler Python API: Cooler.matrix(balance=True).fetch(region) returns a NumPy array; Cooler.pixels(join=True)[:] a table of non-zero pixels. cooltools.expected_cis averages each diagonal (columns dist in bins and balanced.avg). matplotlib imshow with LogNorm gives the usual log colour scale; NaN (masked) bins are white.

B1. (a) Two domains: bins 1–4 and bins 5–8, with the boundary between bins 4 and 5. Within each block the values fall slowly (40 → 22 → 15), between the blocks they are low (≤ 7). (b) Pixel (1, 4) is inside domain 1; pixel (4, 7) crosses the boundary. At the same distance (150 kb), contacts within a domain are about four times more frequent than across a boundary: that is what insulation means. (c) No. At its distance it is high because it is inside a domain, like (1, 4) = 15. A loop is a pixel enriched over its local neighbourhood, including the pixels next to it closer to the diagonal. Here (5, 8) = 18 is lower than its neighbours (5, 7) = 25 and (6, 8) = 24: it is just the far corner of domain 2. A loop would show as a local maximum, e.g. (5, 8) = 40 with (5, 7) and (6, 8) near 25: a dot at the corner of the domain.

B2. (a) 10 times less at 1 Mb, 100 times less at 10 Mb. (b) \(O/E = 60/20 = 3\). The raw count depends mostly on distance: a 500-kb pixel with 60 contacts is special, a 20-kb pixel with 60 contacts may be below average. (c) Mostly not: two positions in the same bin are linearly adjacent, so they are always close. It also collects artefacts such as unligated and self-ligated fragments (removed in processing) and is excluded from most analyses.

B3. (a) Each side of an extruding cohesin stops at the first site pointing towards it: the left side at a → site, the right side at a ← site. Loaded between A and B: loop A–B. Loaded between C and D: loop C–D. Loaded between B and C: the left side passes B (←, wrong way) and stops at A, the right side passes C (→) and stops at D: a larger, weaker loop A–D. The main domains are A–B and C–D; the divergent pair B ← | → C is the boundary between them (a classic strong boundary). (b) The promoter (200 kb) is in domain A–B, the enhancer (600 kb) in domain C–D, on opposite sides of the boundary; they meet only within the weaker A–D loop. Expect little activation. (c) Without C, cohesin loaded anywhere between B and D extrudes until A and D: the C–D domain is replaced by an A–D domain that contains both the promoter and the enhancer (the A–B loop remains nested inside). The enhancer can now reach the gene. (d) With C inverted (←), the right side of cohesin loaded between B and C stops at C: loops A–B, A–C and A–D; there is no longer a → site between A and D apart from A itself, so the boundary between B and C is gone and promoter and enhancer again share a domain. Either change can switch the gene on, which is the logic of the limb malformations in the lecture.

B4. (a) CTCF depleted: domains and loops largely gone (the squares and dots fade, contacts spread across the old boundaries), checkerboard unchanged. Cohesin depleted: all loop domains and dots gone, the near-diagonal looks smooth, the checkerboard becomes stronger and finer. (b) (1) The experiment measures short-term effects (hours to days); maintenance of an existing state can be robust while establishing a new state (development, activation) needs the structure. (2) Many enhancer–promoter contacts are short-range or CTCF-independent and survive. (3) “~200 genes” can include key genes and still be a strong phenotype; a degron in one cell type does not test other cell types or development. Also: residual CTCF, compensation, and expression measured as steady-state RNA (slow) rather than nascent transcription.

B5. (a) \((100^2 + 50^2 + 50^2) / 200^2 = 15\,000 / 40\,000 = 0.375\). (b) 80% ≫ 37.5%: chromosomes form territories. 45% close to random suggests a technical problem: many random ligations between fragments from different nuclei or in solution (poor crosslinking, nuclei lysed before ligation). The cis fraction is a standard Hi-C QC metric.

B6. (a) No. Hi-C counts ligation events summed over cells; 10% of millions of cells in contact gives many reads, far more than at neighbouring pixels where contact is rarer, so a dot appears. FISH shows the same thing as a distribution: most cells far apart, a minority close. (b) “The enhancer and promoter come close (within 200 nm) in about 10% of cells at any moment, more often than neighbouring loci.” Contact is transient and in a minority of cells.

B7. (a) 3C (qPCR across the ligation junction) or 4C from the promoter as viewpoint; cheap per sample. (b) Promoter Capture Hi-C (or Capture-C): baits for 2 000 promoters, deep at those. (c) Hi-C at modest depth (compartments are visible at 100 kb–1 Mb with a few hundred million reads or less). (d) HiChIP or ChIA-PET for cohesin (SMC1/RAD21): reads concentrated at bound anchors. (e) Micro-C: MNase cuts at every linker, giving nucleosome-level resolution. (f) Imaging: DNA-FISH or chromatin tracing (or single-cell Hi-C), because Hi-C averages over cells.

B8. (a) The limb enhancers lose their boundary and gene Y; gene X is now in the same domain as the limb enhancers and can be activated ectopically in the limb (as PAX3 in brachydactyly). (b) Capture-C or 4C from the gene X promoter in patient fibroblasts or limb buds of a mouse model to show new contacts with the enhancers; RNA (RNA-seq or in situ hybridisation) to show ectopic X expression in the limb; a mouse with the same deletion made by CRISPR to test whether it causes the phenotype. (c) Probably not: a deletion inside domain 2 that keeps the boundary does not bring the enhancers to X (Lupiáñez et al.: rewiring only happened when the variant disrupted a CTCF-associated boundary). It could still cause a phenotype by deleting enhancers of Y.
